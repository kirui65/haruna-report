Sifuna warns opposition: deliver change in 90 days or face the streets

WhatsAppFacebookX

The Nairobi senator tells Kenyans in the United States that a post-Ruto government will have no grace period — and that the Linda Mwananchi movement will hold any successor to the same standard it holds the current administration.

Nairobi Senator Edwin Sifuna has warned that an opposition government replacing President William Ruto after the 2027 General Election would have no more than three months to demonstrate meaningful change before Kenyans returned to the streets, setting one of the starkest accountability benchmarks yet articulated by a senior figure in the emerging opposition coalition.

Sifuna delivered the warning during engagements with Kenyans living in the United States, where he has been meeting diaspora communities as part of efforts to build momentum for the Linda Mwananchi movement ahead of next year&#8217;s election. His message was directed as much at his own political allies as at the electorate — a deliberate signal that opposition unity, should it materialise, would carry obligations that did not expire at the ballot box.

&#8220;If we slacken, if we go there and continue with business as usual, it will not take more than three months — these young people will be back in the street saying, &#8216;The government must go,'&#8221; Sifuna said, referencing the Gen Z-led protests that convulsed Kenya in June 2024 and forced Ruto to withdraw the Finance Bill under sustained public pressure.

The reference was pointed and entirely intentional. Those protests — which drew tens of thousands of young Kenyans into the streets of Nairobi, Mombasa, Kisumu and beyond, resulted in approximately 62 deaths according to human rights organisations, and briefly breached the perimeter of Parliament Buildings — fundamentally altered the political landscape of Ruto&#8217;s presidency. They demonstrated, with a clarity that no opinion poll could match, that Kenya&#8217;s youth were prepared to hold governments to account through direct action when they felt that constitutional and electoral processes had failed them. Sifuna was telling his audience, and through them the opposition leadership, that the generation which brought down the Finance Bill would apply exactly the same standard to whoever succeeded Ruto.

It is a position that sets Sifuna apart from much of the opposition discourse currently circulating in Kenya&#8217;s political arena, where the conversation has concentrated heavily on the mechanics of defeating Ruto rather than on what victory would actually require in its immediate aftermath. While former Deputy President Rigathi Gachagua builds his Democracy for the Citizens Party network across the Mt Kenya region, and while various opposition figures jockey for positioning within potential coalition arrangements, Sifuna is insisting that the more urgent question is what a post-Ruto administration would do in its first quarter — and whether opposition leaders have agreed on the answer before asking Kenyans to trust them with power.

His conditions for unity are specific and economically focused. Sifuna said opposition leaders must, before forming a united political front, reach consensus on immediate measures to ease the financial burden on ordinary households. He cited reducing or eliminating taxes on fuel and electricity, and scrapping the housing levy — a mandatory deduction from payrolls that has been among the most bitterly contested elements of the Ruto administration&#8217;s fiscal policy — as priorities that any credible opposition platform must commit to delivering promptly after a change of government.

&#8220;We are not going to government to get comfortable. There will be no room for being comfortable,&#8221; Sifuna said, in remarks that carried an implicit rebuke of political cultures in which opposition movements, having secured power, rapidly accommodated themselves to the same patronage structures and spending habits they had campaigned against.

The senator argued that reducing government expenditure to create fiscal space for tax relief was not merely a policy preference but a precondition for public trust. Kenyans had, in his reading, reached a point of acute impatience with administrations that promised relief and delivered rationalisation. An opposition government that arrived in office and then began explaining why the promised changes were more complicated than anticipated would, he suggested, face a public that had heard the explanation before and was no longer prepared to accept it.

The political context surrounding Sifuna&#8217;s US trip extends beyond his remarks on accountability timelines. His engagements drew attention to a proposal, raised during a meeting with diaspora members on Saturday, that Kenyans living abroad could mobilise resources to support the construction of Jomo Kenyatta International Airport. Participant Kiemo Mohando put the idea directly: &#8220;We can build the airport. Don&#8217;t look for another Adani. We can build an airport. We can reorganise ourselves — let&#8217;s partner with the government, let&#8217;s do the airport.&#8221;

The reference to Adani placed Mohando&#8217;s proposal in an immediate and politically charged context. India&#8217;s Adani Group has featured prominently in debates about Kenya&#8217;s aviation infrastructure, and the broader controversy surrounding the conglomerate&#8217;s international operations — including bribery allegations in the United States — has made any association with Adani infrastructure projects politically sensitive in Kenya. Mohando&#8217;s suggestion that the diaspora could serve as an alternative financing source for JKIA tapped into a genuine appetite within Kenyan communities abroad to participate more directly in national development. It remained, however, a participant&#8217;s proposal rather than a formal commitment by any organised diaspora body, and Sifuna&#8217;s office did not indicate that it had been formally adopted as opposition policy.

What the exchange did illuminate is the degree to which Kenyans outside the country are engaging with the pre-election political debate with an intensity that rival the conversations happening in Nairobi&#8217;s political circles. The diaspora — concentrated in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and the Gulf — represents not only a source of remittances that constitute a significant portion of Kenya&#8217;s foreign exchange earnings, but an increasingly vocal constituency that opposition and government figures alike are competing to address.

For Sifuna, the US engagements served a dual purpose: reinforcing the Linda Mwananchi movement&#8217;s national reach and testing the resonance of a message built on accountability rather than simply on anti-Ruto sentiment. The distinction matters. Kenya has cycled through enough post-election disappointments to have produced a cohort of voters, particularly among the young, who are as sceptical of opposition promises as they are of government performance.

Telling that cohort that a new government would face the streets within 90 days of failing to deliver is either a remarkable act of political honesty or a carefully calibrated piece of positioning — perhaps both simultaneously. In Kenya&#8217;s pre-election landscape, the two are not always easy to distinguish.

What is clear is that the standard Sifuna has articulated publicly now follows the opposition into every conversation it has between now and August 2027. He has set the clock. The question is whether his allies are prepared to run against it.
