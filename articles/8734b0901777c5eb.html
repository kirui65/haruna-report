Botswana Voted Out Its Fifth President for a Lawyer: Will Kenya Follow?

WhatsAppFacebookX

As Kenya's political class sharpens its strategies ahead of the August 2027 General Election, an extraordinary historical comparison between Kenya and Botswana has captured national attention, revealing a sequence of political coincidences spanning more than six decades since both nations gained independence from Britain.

From founding fathers who died in office and deputies who inherited power, to trained economists who ruled for a decade and sons of independence leaders who returned to State House as fourth presidents, the presidential timelines of Nairobi and Gaborone read like parallel chapters written by the same hand.

Now, with Botswana having ended 58 years of single party dominance in October 2024 by voting out its fifth president after a single elected term in favour of a lawyer and human rights advocate, political observers in Kenya are closely examining how the parallel aligns with the rapid rise of Nairobi Senator and Linda Mwananchi leader Edwin Sifuna.

Both Kenya and Botswana emerged from British colonial administration in the mid 1960s under Westminster style parliamentary traditions that later evolved into executive presidencies.

Kenya attained independence from Britain on December 12, 1963, with Jomo Kenyatta becoming Prime Minister and subsequently the country's first President in 1964, while Botswana gained independence from Britain on September 30, 1966, with Sir Seretse Khama taking office as its founding President.

In the first striking parallel, neither founding leader lived to retire from office, as both died while actively serving as head of state.

Kenyatta died in Mombasa on August 22, 1978, after nearly 15 years at the helm, while Seretse Khama died in Gaborone on July 13, 1980, after 14 years in office.

In both capitals, constitutional succession placed power directly into the hands of their sitting deputies, ushering in long tenures marked by centralized party control.

In Kenya, Vice President Daniel arap Moi assumed the presidency in August 1978 and went on to govern for 24 years until December 2002, while in Botswana, Vice President Ketumile Masire succeeded Seretse Khama in July 1980 and ruled for 18 years until March 1998.

When both nations transitioned to their third presidents, voters and ruling establishments turned away from career politicians toward seasoned economists tasked with stabilizing public finances and modernizing institutions.

In Botswana, Festus Mogae, an Oxford trained economist and former Governor of the Bank of Botswana, served as third president for a full ten year period between 1998 and 2008.

Four years after Mogae took office in Gaborone, Kenyans elected Mwai Kibaki, a Makerere and London School of Economics trained economist and former Finance Minister, who served as Kenya's third president for a matching ten year period between 2002 and 2013.

The fourth chapter in both countries produced an even more remarkable coincidence, as each nation elected the son of its founding president as its fourth head of state.

In Botswana, Ian Khama, the eldest son of founding president Sir Seretse Khama, assumed the presidency in April 2008 and served ten years until March 2018.

In Kenya, Uhuru Kenyatta, the son of founding president Jomo Kenyatta, was elected in March 2013 and served two five year terms until September 2022.

Beyond lineage, the political dynamics surrounding the exit of both fourth presidents followed an almost identical script involving bitter fallout with their chosen deputies.

In Botswana, Ian Khama handed over power in 2018 to his Vice President, Mokgweetsi Masisi, who became the country's fifth president, only for an acute political rift to erupt between the two men within months.

Ahead of Botswana's 2019 general election, Khama resigned from the ruling Botswana Democratic Party, which his father had founded, and campaigned openly for the opposition in an effort to unseat Masisi, a bid that fell short as Masisi retained the presidency.

Three years later in Kenya, outgoing fourth president Uhuru Kenyatta fell out publicly with his Deputy President, William Ruto, and threw his full political weight behind opposition leader Raila Odinga in the August 2022 General Election, only for Odinga to lose as Ruto ascended to become Kenya's fifth president.

It is the sixth and most recent chapter in Botswana that has fuelled intense debate across Kenyan political circles and social media platforms.

In October 2024, Botswana voters handed Mokgweetsi Masisi a historic defeat after just one elected five year term, ending nearly six decades of uninterrupted rule by the Botswana Democratic Party and electing opposition leader Duma Boko, a Harvard trained lawyer and prominent human rights advocate, as the country's sixth president.

For Kenyan political commentators, the parallel has gained traction because Kenya has never voted out a sitting president after a single term, with Jomo Kenyatta, Moi, Kibaki, and Uhuru Kenyatta all serving at least ten years in office.

That historical comparison has centred heavily on Nairobi Senator Edwin Sifuna, an Advocate of the High Court whose grassroots mobilization under the Linda Mwananchi movement has propelled him ahead of traditional opposition heavyweights in national opinion polling.

A national survey released by TIFA Research in November 2026 placed Sifuna statistically tied with President Ruto at 31% support each, leaving veteran opposition figures trailing far behind, with Wiper Patriotic Front leader Kalonzo Musyoka at 9%, former Interior Cabinet Secretary Fred Matiang'i at 5%, and Democracy for the Citizens Party leader Rigathi Gachagua at 4%.

Political observers note that Sifuna's youthful energy, legal background, and direct street level mobilization across counties mirror the civic appeal that propelled Duma Boko to victory in Gaborone, particularly among younger voters frustrated by unemployment and the rising cost of living.

At the same time, Kenya's broader opposition bench features several other prominent legal practitioners, including Senior Counsel Kalonzo Musyoka and People's Liberation Party presidential aspirant Martha Karua, underscoring how strongly lawyers dominate the alternative leadership conversation ahead of 2027.

However, analysts caution that while the biographical coincidences between Gaborone and Nairobi are extraordinary, electoral outcomes in Kenya are shaped by distinct local realities, including regional voting blocs, party structures, and opposition unity.

Unlike Botswana, where rival opposition parties coalesced under the Umbrella for Democratic Change to rally behind a single flagbearer in Duma Boko, Kenya's opposition faces the decisive test of resolving internal rivalries and fielding a united front before voters cast their ballots in August 2027.
