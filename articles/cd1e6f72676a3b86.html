Ruto tells Bungoma crowds the 2024 Finance Bill caused no harm

WhatsAppFacebookX

The President&#8217;s claim that Kenya&#8217;s most contested legislation passed without damage collides head-on with a documented death toll of 62 and streets that ran with teargas and blood.

President William Ruto yesterday told crowds in Bungoma County that Kenya&#8217;s 2024 Finance Bill had been passed without harming the nation, attributing the widespread protests it triggered to rival politicians who, he said, spread misinformation to manipulate young Kenyans into taking to the streets.

The remarks, delivered during a public function at which the President also disbursed sugarcane bonuses to local farmers, represent his most direct attempt yet to reframe the defining political crisis of his administration ahead of the 2027 General Election. They were also, within hours, among the most contested statements he has made since taking office — and the speed and fury of the public response suggested that the reframing had not landed as smoothly as his advisers might have hoped.

&#8220;The 2024 Finance Bill was eventually passed, and there was no harm to our nation,&#8221; Ruto told the gathering, his tone measured and confident. He then addressed the generation whose mobilisation nearly derailed his government&#8217;s fiscal agenda directly, with evident assurance: &#8220;Gen Zs wananipenda — hawatakubali kugonganishwa na Sifuna na Gachagua vile walifanya 2024.&#8221; Translated: &#8220;Gen Z loves me — they will not allow themselves to be used against me by Sifuna and Gachagua the way they were in 2024.&#8221;

The political calculation behind those words is not difficult to read. With former Deputy President Rigathi Gachagua now openly building an opposition movement through his Democracy for the Citizens Party, and Orange Democratic Movement Secretary-General Edwin Sifuna maintaining a consistent and pointed critique of the administration, Ruto is working methodically to detach Kenya&#8217;s youth from the political figures he regards as his principal threats heading into 2027. Telling Gen Z that they were manipulated rather than motivated — that their anger in 2024 was manufactured by opportunistic politicians rather than rooted in genuine grievance — is a strategy aimed at neutralising what became, in June of that year, the most significant spontaneous political mobilisation Kenya had witnessed in a generation.

The historical record, however, is not easily revised. What began as an online campaign against specific tax proposals embedded in the Finance Bill 2024 evolved, within weeks, into a nationwide movement that drew tens of thousands of young Kenyans into the streets of Nairobi, Mombasa, Kisumu, Nakuru and dozens of other towns simultaneously. On 25 June 2024, protesters breached the perimeter of Parliament Buildings in Nairobi in scenes broadcast live around the world. Security forces responded with live ammunition. By any credible account — including documentation compiled by Amnesty International Kenya and the Kenya Human Rights Commission — approximately 62 people died during the protests and their aftermath. Hundreds more were injured. Dozens were abducted in the days that followed, and a significant number remain unaccounted for.

Ruto subsequently withdrew the Finance Bill in its entirety under that sustained pressure — an extraordinary concession from a sitting president that acknowledged, implicitly and unmistakably, the potency of the opposition he faced. The withdrawal was presented by the government at the time as a demonstration of democratic responsiveness, a leader listening to his people. He later enacted a number of the bill&#8217;s revenue-raising provisions through alternative legislative and administrative mechanisms — a move that critics said achieved through the back door what had been emphatically rejected at the front. Yesterday&#8217;s remarks in Bungoma appeared to retroactively recast that entire episode as something closer to a tactical pause in the face of manufactured chaos, rather than a genuine retreat before legitimate public anger.

Social media users reacted with barely contained fury. Screenshots of teargas canisters fired directly into crowds, images of protesters bleeding on the streets outside Parliament, photographs of candlelit vigils held for those who did not come home, and carefully compiled lists of the confirmed dead circulated rapidly across X, Facebook and TikTok within hours of Ruto&#8217;s comments being broadcast nationally. For many Kenyans who had marched, who had lost friends, siblings or colleagues, or who had simply watched events unfold in real time from their phones, the suggestion that no harm had been done was not a political reframing they were willing to absorb. It was, in the words of several users whose posts gathered tens of thousands of engagements, an insult dressed as history.

The anger is entirely comprehensible. But the political logic underpinning Ruto&#8217;s approach, viewed from his own strategic position, is not irrational. The President faces a 2027 election in which the youth vote — Kenya&#8217;s largest and fastest-growing demographic bloc, with millions of first-time voters registered since the 2022 cycle — will almost certainly be decisive. If that generation can be persuaded that their 2024 mobilisation was the product of elite manipulation rather than legitimate fury at government policy, the administration neutralises one of the most powerful arguments his opponents can deploy on the campaign trail. If they cannot be persuaded, those same voters represent perhaps the most consequential electoral threat Ruto has faced since taking office.

Bungoma itself was chosen with care. The western Kenya county sits in sugarcane country, and the bonus disbursements Ruto oversaw during the same visit addressed a specific and longstanding grievance — delayed payments from millers that have damaged farming livelihoods across the region for years. That concrete delivery, money placed visibly and directly into farmers&#8217; hands, is precisely the kind of tangible governance action the administration hopes will carry more weight with rural voters than opposition rhetoric. It is a campaign approach Ruto has deployed consistently and with some success: appear in a county, deliver a benefit, challenge opponents to match it and move on before the criticism catches up.

The Finance Bill remark overshadowed the farmer narrative entirely. By volunteering a claim so directly and so publicly contradicted by documented events — events that most Kenyans lived through, watched or participated in — Ruto handed his opponents material they were quick to deploy. Gachagua&#8217;s allies circulated the statement alongside footage of the protests within the hour. Sifuna responded publicly, calling the President&#8217;s characterisation an insult to families who had buried their children after confronting a government that had, in his telling, chosen revenue over lives.

The deeper question the episode raises concerns the nature of political truth in Kenya&#8217;s accelerating pre-election environment. With August 2027 now less than nine months away, every major public appearance by the President, every rally convened by Gachagua, every statement from the emerging opposition coalition will be examined not merely for factual accuracy but for what it signals about the narratives being tested and the alliances being assembled. In that environment, Ruto&#8217;s Bungoma remarks are less a historical claim than a campaign opening move — a deliberate declaration that he intends to contest the meaning of 2024 as vigorously as he will contest the ballot of 2027.

Whether that contest can be won depends, in substantial part, on whether the young Kenyans he addressed yesterday are prepared to accept the version of events being offered to them. The difficulty for the President is that this particular audience was not receiving information second-hand. They were there. They streamed it live. They carried the placards. They attended the funerals.

No political speech, however confident its delivery, can entirely overwrite a memory that was made in the streets, in the teargas and in the grief that followed.
