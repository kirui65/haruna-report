Kenyan Researchers Secure Funding to Study Climate’S Impact on Health

WhatsAppFacebookX

Scientists led by the Kenya Medical Research Institute (KEMRI) have received £2.3 million (about KSh 394 million) to study how heat, drought, malaria, snakebite, hunger and mental distress pile up on the same households in Turkana County. The researchers say these overlapping burdens have gone largely unmeasured.

The three-year study, Strategic Health Integration for Equitable and Localized Decision-making (SHIELD), is funded by the UK-based Wellcome Trust.

&#8220;A single family may face heat from outdoor work and a poorly insulated home, food and water shortages, mosquitoes breeding around water points, and snakes in grazing and water-collection areas, all at the same time,&#8221; said Dr Daniel Kwaro, the study&#8217;s principal investigator.

Dr Kwaro said that, to the best of his knowledge, this is the first project to consider these risks together, because health and weather systems record them separately. Drought is logged without information on who is exposed, and clinics record illness without its link to the climate.

&#8220;Heat illness, from fainting to heatstroke, is rarely recorded as heat illness, and even when a clinic sees it, the link to the weather is usually lost,&#8221; Dr Kwaro said.

Dr Patrick Amoth, Director General for Health, said the government needs this evidence because the country&#8217;s health information systems are built to count diseases one at a time.

&#8220;If we, as a government, cannot see that burden, we cannot plan or budget for it, or act before it becomes a crisis,&#8221; Dr Amoth said at the launch of the project today in Nairobi.

Over three years, researchers will survey refugee and host households across four seasons, fit 100 adults with smartwatches to measure heat strain, trap mosquitoes monthly, survey snakes in areas where people are bitten, and compare clinic records with what households report. With Turkana County and humanitarian agencies, the team will turn the findings into a climate-health dashboard, a budgeting tool and an early-warning system that can help governments plan.

Dr Gilchrist Lokoel, Turkana County&#8217;s Chief Officer of Health, told reporters at the launch that the county was waiting for the evidence, given the sharp changes it has seen in drought, heat and heat-related illness.

Malaria is one of the risks the study will track. Dr Eric Ochomo, head of entomology at KEMRI and a member of the SHIELD team, said a rise of about one degree Celsius had let mosquitoes thrive in places where they were not traditionally found. Anopheles stephensi , a malaria mosquito native to South Asia and the Middle East, was first confirmed in Kenya in 2022 and has since been found in 13 counties, he said. Dr Ochomo said the response would need community awareness as well as science.

&#8220;Covering tanks, screening inlets and draining spillage at tap stands and boreholes would stop stored water from breeding mosquitoes, and that work belongs in malaria control, including surveillance for species of mosquitoes, such as the Anopheles stephensi,&#8221; Dr Ochomo said.

The researchers describe Turkana as a &#8220;sentinel&#8221; setting. It does not represent the whole country or continent, but it lets them study how overlapping risks build up, with lessons for similar dryland and refugee-hosting areas.
