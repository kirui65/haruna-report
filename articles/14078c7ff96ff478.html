Kenya’s small farmers feed the nation but are losing the battle

WhatsAppFacebookX

From climate shocks to exploitative cartels, Kenya&#8217;s smallholder farmers face a perfect storm — and without urgent intervention, the country&#8217;s food security hangs in the balance.

Kenya&#8217;s agricultural sector feeds millions, drives the economy, and sustains entire communities — yet the very farmers who make this possible are being left behind.

Agriculture is the backbone of Kenya&#8217;s economy. More than 70 per cent of Kenyans living in rural areas depend on farming for food and income, most of them smallholder families cultivating land by hand, selling modest surpluses, and hoping the rains arrive on time. For these families, farming is not a livelihood choice — it is survival. It puts food on the table, pays school fees, and keeps households afloat in an economy where formal employment remains out of reach for most. Beyond the household, agriculture feeds industry. Tea factories, sugar mills, cotton processors, and food manufacturers all draw their raw materials from Kenya&#8217;s farms. The government, in turn, taxes these industries, channelling revenue into roads, schools, and public services. The chain is long and the stakes are high.

Yet for all its importance, farming in Kenya is in crisis — and the people bearing the heaviest burden are the smallholders who do the most work and earn the least reward.

Climate change has fundamentally altered the rhythms that generations of Kenyan farmers relied upon. Rainfall patterns have become unpredictable. Prolonged droughts wipe out entire harvests. Flash floods destroy crops overnight. Farmers who once planted by the calendar now plant by guesswork, and many lose everything. Most Kenyans, it should be noted, combine farming with other work — tending a small shamba alongside a job in town or a business in the market — which means that when harvests fail, the financial blow ripples through entire households and communities.

Equally damaging is the stranglehold of middlemen and cartels. At harvest time, brokers descend on farms and buy produce at exploitatively low prices. They warehouse it, wait for scarcity to push prices up, and then sell at a premium — pocketing profits that should, by rights, belong to the farmer who planted, tended, and harvested the crop. The farmer works; the middleman earns. This cycle has persisted for decades, and it has kept smallholders poor.

The cost of farm inputs compounds the problem. Seeds, fertiliser, agrochemicals, and equipment have become prohibitively expensive, partly due to taxation and importation costs. Many farmers cannot afford mechanisation and fall back on hand hoes and traditional methods that limit both productivity and income. Meanwhile, cheap imported produce — often subsidised in its country of origin — floods Kenyan markets and undercuts local farmers who cannot compete on price. Small-scale producers are squeezed from every direction: high costs of production, low sale prices, and foreign competition they have no tools to fight.

There are also tensions closer to home. In many parts of Kenya, conflict between crop farmers and pastoralists is a recurring and destructive reality. Livestock straying onto cultivated land destroys harvests and ignites bitter disputes between neighbours, sometimes with lasting consequences for community relations and farm productivity.

None of these challenges are insurmountable. Solutions exist, but they require coordinated commitment from farmers, government, and the broader community.

Irrigation must replace dependence on rainfall. The government has the technical capacity to expand irrigation infrastructure and the responsibility to do so urgently. Alongside this, farmers should be supported to adopt drought-resistant crop varieties and given timely, accurate weather forecasts via radio and mobile platforms — tools that are affordable and already widely accessible.

Cooperative farming offers the most practical antidote to middlemen and cartels. When farmers sell collectively, they negotiate from strength. Cooperatives can also pool resources to purchase inputs at wholesale prices, reducing individual costs significantly. Organic manure — readily available on most smallholder farms — can substitute for expensive chemical fertiliser, cutting costs further while improving soil health.

The government must do its part on trade policy. Meaningful protection for local farmers — through controlled imports and reduced input taxes — would level a playing field that is currently tilted heavily against the smallholder. At the same time, farmers need training and support to improve product quality: better packaging, grading, and processing that command higher market prices and open doors to formal retail and export markets.

Technology is already transforming farming for those who can access it. Mobile phones allow farmers to monitor weather, track market prices, and sell directly to buyers without intermediaries. Mechanisation, where affordable, dramatically increases productivity. The government and development partners must work together to close the gap between farmers who have access to these tools and the many who do not.

Kenya&#8217;s small-scale farmers are not a charity case. They are the foundation of the country&#8217;s food system, its rural economy, and its social fabric. They deserve policy that protects them, markets that reward them, and a government that takes their challenges as seriously as their contribution.

If Kenya is to feed itself in the decades ahead, it must start by ensuring the people who grow the food can afford to keep farming.
