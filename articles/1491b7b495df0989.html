Kenya Construction Regulator Flags Inspection Gaps After 72 Collapses

WhatsAppFacebookX

Kenya&#x27;s National Construction Authority covers only 27 counties with five Nairobi inspectors after 72 building collapses in three years, amid heavy rains.

Kenya's National Construction Authority has warned that severe underfunding leaves it operating in only 27 of the country's 47 counties, even after 72 buildings collapsed nationwide over the past three years.

According to a November 19, 2026 Citizen TV report and a November 20 Kenyans.co.ke update, the regulator faces acute staffing constraints as seasonal heavy rainfall heightens structural risks across active construction zones. In Nairobi, the epicentre of Kenya's high-rise development boom, only five NCA inspection officers are tasked with overseeing thousands of ongoing projects.

The disclosure exposes a central contradiction in Kenya's built environment: while the state replaced a 56-year-old colonial building order in February 2025 with the National Building Code, 2024, front-line capacity to verify whether developers follow those rules has fallen far behind the pace of urban construction.

Leaving 20 Kenyan counties without an NCA operational footprint forces regulators to stretch sparse teams across wide regions or rely on reactive checks after failures occur. With only five inspectors assigned to Nairobi's entire construction pipeline, routine site verification across excavation, foundation curing, concrete mixing, and multi-storey framing becomes physically impossible at scale.

The human stakes of inspection gaps were underscored in Eastleigh, Nairobi, where an 18-storey building collapsed in October 2026. On November 5, the fourteenth day of search and rescue operations, Citizen TV reported that teams recovered six additional bodies, bringing the confirmed death toll to eight while five people remained unaccounted for and families waited for answers.

Increased rainfall across Kenya adds immediate physical stress to compromised structures. Waterlogged soils reduce bearing capacity under shallow or poorly engineered foundations, while unsealed concrete slabs and inadequate drainage accelerate structural degradation on stalled or hurried sites.

Preventing structural failures requires coordination across multiple laws and institutions rather than a single agency acting alone. Under Kenya's statutory framework, responsibility is divided between county governments and national regulators at distinct stages of a project's life cycle.

In practice, that split creates dangerous seams. County governments issue initial development permissions and approve architectural and structural plans under the Physical and Land Use Planning Act. The NCA, established under Act No. 41 of 2011, registers contractors, accredits site supervisors and skilled workers, registers projects, and monitors compliance on active sites.

When county plan-vetting, NCA site registration, and physical inspections fail to align, unauthorised floors, unverified design changes, or unapproved occupancy can persist undetected until a structure fails. Published in Kenya Gazette No.

28 on March 1, 2024 and commenced on February 20, 2025, the National Building Code, 2024 replaced the 1968 Local Government Building Order with updated rules on structural design, fire protection, electrical installations, occupancy certification, and enforcement. Yet updated legal standards cannot inspect concrete pours on their own.

Even when inspectors flag violations, the gap between legal authority and sustained enforcement remains wide across fast-growing neighbourhoods. Developers face intense commercial pressure to finish multi-storey residential blocks quickly and occupy ground floors before upper levels or certification procedures are complete.

Public records reviewed from the November 19 and November 20 briefings do not disclose the exact size of the NCA's current funding shortfall in shillings, nor has the regulator published a county-by-county breakdown identifying where the 72 collapses occurred or how many resulted in criminal convictions.

Under the National Construction Authority Act, contractors, site supervisors, and skilled construction workers must hold accreditation, and ongoing projects must be registered before works proceed. However, without enough county offices or inspectors to verify compliance on the ground, registration risks remaining a paper formality rather than an active safety filter.

For prospective tenants, buyers, and lenders, the practical baseline is clear: verifying both county development approvals and NCA project registration before committing money remains essential, yet self-checks cannot substitute for state inspections. Until Kenya matches its 2024 Building Code with funded inspectors across all 47 counties, heavy rains and rapid high-rise construction will continue to test weak links in the regulatory chain.
