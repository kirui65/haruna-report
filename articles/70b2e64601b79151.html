Kenyan students and their families are increasingly falling prey to fraudulent study-abroad agents who exploit the growing demand for overseas education.

This has prompted regulators to issue fresh warnings about the scale of the problem.

The Commission for University Education (CUE), empowered under the Universities Act, mandates that all student recruitment agencies operating in Kenya must be duly registered, vetted and licensed.

CUE publicly maintains a register of approved consultants, searchable by name, branch and licence expiry date.

Any agent whose name does not appear on that list, or whose licence cannot be independently verified, is operating illegally.

CUE has also previously warned that degrees obtained from universities not on its approved list will not be recognised in Kenya.

Beyond the licence check, students should be alert to agents who claim guaranteed admission, visas or scholarships.

No legitimate agent anywhere in the world can promise a visa outcome; that decision rests solely with the relevant embassy based on official criteria.

For example, the US and Canada&#039;s immigration authorities have stated plainly that any offer of a "special visa deal" or "guaranteed approval" is a fraud.

Requests for large upfront payments sent to personal M-Pesa numbers or private bank accounts are a consistent warning sign.

Genuine fees should be paid directly to the university or the relevant government portal, and official receipts on company letterhead must be provided for every transaction.

Other warning signs include offers of fully-funded scholarships that require an application fee (legitimate scholarships are free to apply for), unfamiliar or unaccredited institutions named in offer letters, and study packages that emphasise full-time work over education.

Student visas almost universally restrict working hours, so any pitch centred on guaranteed employment abroad should be treated with deep scepticism.

Agents who apply high-pressure tactics, such as claiming only a few spots remain or demanding payment within hours, are using classic manipulation techniques designed to prevent students from doing proper research.

Perhaps the most dangerous sign is an agent who encourages a student to submit false bank statements, forged transcripts or fabricated sponsor letters.

Using fraudulent documents can result in permanent visa bans, regardless of whether the student was aware of the falsification.

If fraud is suspected, students should stop all payments immediately, preserve all evidence, including screenshots and receipts, and file a report with the Directorate of Criminal Investigations (DCI).

CUE should also be notified if the agent claimed to be licensed. Applying directly through a university&#039;s official website, without any agent, remains the safest option available.
