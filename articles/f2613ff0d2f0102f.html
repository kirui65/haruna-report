Kenya firms lose KSh95.5B yearly to workplace harassment, CEOs told

WhatsAppFacebookX

A World Bank-convened roundtable in Nairobi has put the staggering cost of gender-based violence and harassment squarely on the boardroom agenda — and the numbers demand a response.

Kenya&#8217;s private sector is haemorrhaging KSh95.5 billion every year to gender-based violence and harassment in the workplace — and the executives who can stop it were in the same room on Thursday to hear exactly what it is costing them.

Business leaders gathered in Nairobi for a CEO Roundtable on Advancing Inclusive Workplaces, convened jointly by the World Bank Group, the Federation of Kenya Employers, and the Kenya Private Sector Alliance. The forum brought together senior corporate decision-makers to confront a crisis hiding in plain sight: workplace harassment that destroys productivity, drives away talent, and drains company resources on a scale that few boardrooms have fully reckoned with.

The figures are stark. A 2025 study by the International Finance Corporation — the World Bank Group&#8217;s private sector arm — found that gender-based violence and harassment costs Kenya&#8217;s private sector an estimated KSh67B in lost productivity and a further KSh28.5B in human resource expenses annually. The study, which examined 26 companies, found that 33.2 per cent of employees had experienced harassment, while a staggering 86 per cent of incidents went unreported. Those affected lost an average of 24.3 working days per year — nearly a month of productive output, quietly disappearing from company ledgers.

Gillian Rogers, IFC&#8217;s Principal Country Officer for Kenya, opened the roundtable by framing workplace inclusion not as a social obligation but as an economic imperative. &#8220;Together, we can build workplaces where women enter, stay, progress, and lead — where companies can draw on the full strength of their talent, and where inclusion translates into stronger people, stronger businesses, and a more competitive Kenyan economy,&#8221; she said.

The discussions ranged across the structural barriers that keep women from advancing in Kenya&#8217;s corporate sector. Women currently hold approximately 40 per cent of entry-level private sector roles, a figure that falls to 28 per cent at the C-suite level and drops further to just 14 per cent among chief executive officers. That narrowing pipeline is not accidental. It is the cumulative effect of harassment, unequal promotion practices, inadequate parental support, and institutional cultures that reward conformity over capability.

Childcare emerged as a particularly significant factor. Women in sub-Saharan Africa carry two to three times more unpaid care work than men, a burden that consistently limits career progression and forces women to make choices between professional advancement and family responsibility. IFC evidence presented at the roundtable demonstrates that employer-supported childcare reduces absenteeism and measurably improves productivity — a finding that reframes childcare not as a welfare provision but as a sound business investment.

Jacqueline Mugo, Executive Director and Chief Executive Officer of the Federation of Kenya Employers, was direct about the corporate cost of inaction. &#8220;Employers have a responsibility to create workplaces where employees feel safe, are treated fairly and have opportunities to grow. When businesses lose experienced workers because of harassment, unfair practices or inadequate support, they also lose valuable skills and productivity,&#8221; she said.

The business case for gender diversity in leadership was reinforced by international evidence shared at the roundtable. Companies with gender-diverse executive teams are 25 per cent more likely to outperform financially, while organisations with diverse management structures report 19 per cent higher innovation revenue. The correlation between inclusion and commercial performance is no longer a matter of debate — it is documented and consistent.

Carole Kariuki, Chief Executive Officer of the Kenya Private Sector Alliance, called on company leaders to do more than invest in women at entry level. &#8220;Businesses are investing in developing talented women, but too few are progressing into senior leadership. We need CEOs to take greater responsibility for removing these barriers, strengthening promotion pathways and ensuring that gender equality becomes part of how businesses measure performance and growth,&#8221; she said.

The World Bank Group has urged Kenyan companies to adopt EDGE certification — an internationally recognised standard for assessing gender equality across pay equity, leadership representation, and career progression — as a mechanism for holding organisations accountable and tracking measurable progress.

For Kenya&#8217;s private sector, the message from Thursday&#8217;s roundtable was unambiguous: workplace inclusion is not a corporate social responsibility line item. It is a financial liability when ignored, and a competitive advantage when embraced. The KSh95.5B annual cost of getting it wrong is no longer a figure that any responsible board can afford to overlook.
