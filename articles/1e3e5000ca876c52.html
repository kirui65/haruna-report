Kenya’s AI Opportunity Depends on Building, Not Just Using

WhatsAppFacebookX

Kenya has built a strong reputation as one of Africa&#8217;s leading technology and innovation hubs. But the next phase of that story will depend on whether we become more than users of artificial intelligence tools developed elsewhere. We risk becoming highly active consumers of AI without developing enough of the technical capacity to shape it, adapt it and build solutions that respond to our own challenges.

Across Nairobi and other major towns, businesses are already using generative AI to draft content, analyse information, automate customer service and improve productivity. Students and professionals are also adopting AI for research, learning and everyday work. This rapid uptake is important, but adoption alone will not determine whether Kenya captures the full economic value of AI.

The more important question is whether we are developing enough people who can create, test, deploy and govern AI systems.

Using an AI tool and engineering a reliable AI solution require very different capabilities. A bank deploying AI for fraud detection, a hospital using it to support diagnostics, an agribusiness applying it to crop forecasting or a logistics company using it to optimise routes needs more than employees who know how to prompt a chatbot.

These organisations need software engineers, data professionals, machine-learning practitioners, cybersecurity specialists and product teams who can turn AI into working systems. They also need people who understand the sectors in which those systems will operate.

That combination matters in Kenya. A model trained on data from another market may not automatically understand local languages, informal business practices, rural connectivity constraints or the realities of our financial and public-service systems. Technical skills must therefore be matched with local context and domain knowledge.

Kenya&#8217;s opportunity is not simply to adopt technologies developed elsewhere, but to build the capacity to adapt them, improve them and develop solutions that respond to our own challenges.

Building AI that works and can be trusted

Developing AI systems is only part of the challenge. We must also be able to establish whether they work as intended.

AI systems can produce inaccurate, biased or unexpected outputs. Organisations need people who can test and evaluate models, identify weaknesses, monitor performance and understand when human judgement must remain in control. These processes should be part of the development and deployment cycle, not an afterthought once a solution is already in use.

Governance will also become a core business capability. As AI becomes embedded in banking, insurance, healthcare, education, marketing and government services, organisations will need stronger processes around privacy, security, accountability and responsible use. Kenya&#8217;s Data Protection Act has already made data governance an important consideration for organisations. AI raises the stakes further, particularly as automated systems become involved in decisions that affect people&#8217;s lives and livelihoods.

The ability to build AI solutions must therefore go hand in hand with the ability to evaluate their impact and use them responsibly.

Turning local capability into economic value

Kenya already has areas where this capability could create significant value: agricultural productivity, financial inclusion, healthcare access, education, manufacturing, mobility and public-service delivery.

However, locally relevant AI solutions are more likely to succeed when the teams building them understand both the technology and the Kenyan context. This means developing professionals who can identify real problems, work with relevant data, design appropriate solutions and take them through testing and deployment.

It also means recognising that AI capability is not developed through exposure to tools alone. Short introductions to AI have value, but they are not enough to build the depth of expertise organisations need. Professionals require practical learning that includes real data, live projects and exposure to the full cycle, from experimentation and development to testing, deployment and ongoing evaluation.

This is where education and training providers, employers and the wider technology ecosystem have an important role to play. At Moringa, we believe technology training must remain grounded in practical application and the ability to solve real-world problems. As the demand for AI-related capabilities grows, developing talent that can move beyond using tools to building and improving solutions will become increasingly important.

Building for the next phase of Kenya&#8217;s AI journey

Kenya does not need to choose between using global AI platforms and building local capability. We need both. Access to global tools can accelerate innovation and productivity, while local expertise enables us to adapt those tools, develop relevant solutions and address the risks that come with their use.

Employers must invest in developing the technical capabilities within their teams and ensure that AI solutions are properly tested and governed before and after deployment. Education and training providers must strengthen practical learning and work more closely with industry to prepare people for the changing demands of the technology sector. Government must continue supporting an environment that encourages responsible innovation, strengthens data protection and creates room for local research and development.

The long-term advantage will belong to organisations and economies that can do more than adopt AI. It will belong to those that can understand it, adapt it and build with it.

Timothy Laku is the Chief Skilling Advisor at Moringa.
