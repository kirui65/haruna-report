Development Is Not Charity: Taxation, Patronage and the Politics of Power in Kenya

WhatsAppFacebookX

Development is not a presidential gift; it is a public obligation funded by the labour and taxes of the people. Yet Kenya&#8217;s political landscape increasingly presents public development as the personal generosity of President William Ruto, who travels across the country announcing projects in ways that often blur the distinction between public responsibility and electoral mobilisation.

From a historical materialist perspective, this is not merely a question of political rhetoric but of power, ownership and the distribution of public resources. The state collects taxes from workers, peasants, traders and businesses across the country. These resources are pooled into the national treasury and must serve the collective needs of the population, not the electoral interests of an individual or political faction.

Walter Rodney, in his final speech, Sign of the Times, delivered on 6 June 1980, challenged the separation of material needs from political rights. His argument that access to milk and constitutional freedom were inseparable reminds us that material conditions and political power are interconnected. In Kenya, access to roads, hospitals, water, schools and electricity cannot be reduced to presidential generosity; these are necessities financed through collective taxation and owed to the people.

The politics of announcing projects during presidential tours risks reproducing a patronage system in which communities are encouraged to associate public investment with political loyalty. Development then becomes a political currency, used to cultivate support, consolidate power and influence electoral outcomes. This obscures the fact that taxpayers are financing these projects in the first place.

The contradiction is particularly glaring in a country where taxation continues to burden ordinary citizens amid rising living costs. Workers and small businesses contribute to the public purse, yet they are often politically positioned as beneficiaries of the very resources they collectively generate. Such a narrative reinforces the illusion that development flows from the generosity of political leaders rather than from the productive labour and financial contributions of society.

Development should not be a reward for voting for the ruling party, attending presidential rallies or endorsing a re-election campaign. It must be guided by public need, equitable distribution, transparency and democratic accountability.

The struggle, therefore, is not simply over which region receives a road or a water project. It is about challenging the politics of patronage that turns public obligations into political favours.

The President is not the benefactor of the people; he is the custodian of public resources. Development is not charity. It is the people&#8217;s right.

Wachira Nyaga is a cadre of the Revolutionary Socialist League, a Founding Member of Social Justice Movement Kenya, a member of Kenya Organic Intellectuals, and the Founder of Embu Community Justice Center. His work focuses on political education, community organising, social justice, African liberation, and the relationship between theory and practice.
