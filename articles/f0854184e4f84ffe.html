Gachagua vs IEBC: The eligibility battle shaping Kenya 2027

WhatsAppFacebookX

Former Deputy President Rigathi Gachagua has turned his contested presidential eligibility into a political weapon — and the IEBC&#8217;s nervous response suggests the question will define Kenya&#8217;s most consequential electoral season in a decade.

Former Deputy President Rigathi Gachagua has publicly challenged the Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission over its handling of his eligibility to contest the 2027 presidential election, escalating a constitutional dispute that has become one of the defining fault lines of Kenya&#8217;s political season and that shows no sign of resolution before the August 2027 General Election.

The confrontation crystallised on 25 November 2026, when the IEBC issued a clarifying statement distancing itself from media reports alleging it had cleared Gachagua to contest, stressing that it had neither approved his candidacy nor made any official determination regarding his legal eligibility. &#8220;The Commission wishes to clarify that it has made no such determination,&#8221; IEBC chairman Erastus Ethekon said, adding that any formal assessment of Gachagua&#8217;s eligibility would only be undertaken at the legally designated stage of the electoral calendar.

Gachagua, characteristically, was not prepared to accept that framing quietly. The Democracy for the Citizens Party leader has consistently maintained that his impeachment from the deputy presidency in October 2024 does not bar him from seeking the country&#8217;s highest office. &#8220;Am I interested? Why not? I am a candidate to be president of the Republic of Kenya. I am qualified. I have support. I am looking for more support, and I&#8217;ll be on the ballot as a candidate for president,&#8221; he has said.

The legal argument underpinning his position is not without substance, and it has attracted senior legal endorsement. Senior Counsel Charles Kanjama, President of the Law Society of Kenya, has argued that Gachagua remains eligible to contest for public office because the appellate process has not been exhausted, pointing to Articles 73, 75 and 99 of the Constitution. The High Court upheld Gachagua&#8217;s impeachment in June 2026, but his legal team filed an appeal, and that pending appeal is central to the eligibility argument his supporters are advancing.

People&#8217;s Liberation Party leader Martha Karua has also defended Gachagua&#8217;s eligibility, arguing that the law allows an individual to remain eligible for office until all appeals have been determined. &#8220;So long as he has given a notice of appeal, you can&#8217;t take away eligibility,&#8221; Karua said, drawing a pointed historical comparison: &#8220;How did Ruto and Uhuru run for president when they were facing Hague charges?&#8221;

The parallel is deliberate and politically loaded. President William Ruto and former President Uhuru Kenyatta both contested and won elections while facing charges at the International Criminal Court — a precedent that Gachagua and his allies cite as evidence that Kenya&#8217;s constitutional framework does not extinguish electoral rights simply because legal proceedings are ongoing. Gachagua has made the same comparison himself. &#8220;Uhuru Kenyatta and Ruto ran for office while they had cases at The Hague. Kenya&#8217;s Constitution is clear that if you have a pending court case, your right to contest for a seat cannot be taken away. So if I want to run, I will run,&#8221; he said.

The counter-argument is equally grounded in law. Ferdinand Waititu&#8217;s attempt to run for governor in 2022 was thwarted by the IEBC after a High Court ruling confirmed his ineligibility due to impeachment — a precedent that those opposing Gachagua&#8217;s candidacy regard as the more directly applicable comparison. The distinction between the ICC cases, which involved criminal charges, and impeachment, which carries a specific constitutional disqualification provision, is one that Kenya&#8217;s courts will ultimately have to resolve.

That resolution may not come before the electoral calendar demands a definitive answer. The IEBC has confirmed that registration of candidates for the 2027 General Election has not yet commenced and that eligibility decisions will be made at the appropriate stage of the process in accordance with the law. The commission&#8217;s studied neutrality is constitutionally defensible, but politically, it leaves a vacuum that both sides are filling aggressively.

Gachagua&#8217;s frustration with the IEBC, however, extends well beyond the eligibility question. He has accused the commission of harbouring a plot to rig the 2027 election using technology from a South Korean company, claiming the firm bungled elections in the Democratic Republic of Congo, the Philippines and Iraq. &#8220;IEBC should tell Kenyans what the role of Mining Cabinet Secretary Hassan Joho and IEBC Vice Chairperson Fahima Arafat is in this tendering system,&#8221; Gachagua said, alleging the tender was designed to favour a particular company. The IEBC has not publicly addressed those specific allegations.

Gachagua commands a significant voting bloc in Mount Kenya and is believed to have access to a sizeable campaign war chest. His political potency is not seriously disputed, even by his opponents. What is disputed — and what will shape the nature of the opposition coalition heading into 2027 — is whether he will be on the ballot at all, or whether he will exercise his considerable influence as a kingmaker rather than a candidate.

He has emphasised that the opposition must present a single candidate to challenge President Ruto in 2027, highlighting the need to address corruption and end extrajudicial killings, while expressing confidence that the 2027 election would be free and fair despite his concerns about the IEBC&#8217;s composition. The tension in that position is obvious: a man who insists he will be a presidential candidate while simultaneously calling for a unified opposition ticket is either building leverage for a negotiated withdrawal or genuinely pursuing both objectives simultaneously.

The IEBC sits uncomfortably at the intersection of these competing pressures. It has walked out on opposition parties during consultative meetings, been accused of institutional bias by Gachagua&#8217;s coalition and been defended by the ruling Kenya Kwanza camp as a legitimate and independent body doing its job. Its credibility with the opposition is already fractured — and the eligibility dispute, whatever its legal resolution, is widening that fracture.

For Kenya&#8217;s democratic architecture, the stakes of getting this right are significant. A situation in which a major presidential aspirant is excluded from the ballot by the IEBC before his appeals have been heard by the Court of Appeal would provoke a constitutional crisis that the country can ill afford in the months before a general election. A situation in which someone constitutionally ineligible nevertheless appears on the ballot would be equally damaging to institutional credibility.

The courts will decide the eligibility question. The IEBC will implement whatever they decide. But the political temperature generated by the dispute — the accusations, the walkouts, the threats of demonstrations — will shape the character of Kenya&#8217;s 2027 campaign long before a single vote is cast.

Gachagua has turned a legal question into a political battleground. In Kenya, that has always been how elections are truly won and lost.
