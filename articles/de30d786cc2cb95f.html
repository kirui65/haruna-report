The Standard questions Ruto’s claims and the meaning of progress

WhatsAppFacebookX

The Standard’s two critiques question both the evidence behind the President’s claims and whether headline achievements reflect rights, security and household costs.

President William Ruto’s State of the Nation address on November 18 left two questions in The Standard’s coverage: whether the figures behind the government’s claims can be checked, and whether those claims describe progress in people’s lives.

The Standard’s two opinion pieces approach the address differently. One takes on the arithmetic. The other challenges the idea that a list of achievements, even if accurate, is enough to show that the country is moving in the right direction. Both say the account presented by the President did not adequately reflect Kenyans’ experience.

The more detailed critique calls for Parliament to revisit a claim Ruto made in November 2025 that the 2024 maize harvest was 67 million bags . The Standard says Citizen TV compared that figure with a Kenya National Bureau of Statistics total of 44.7 million, leaving a gap of 22.3 million bags. The article asks whether the numbers used comparable methods and calls on the Agriculture Cabinet Secretary to explain the source and measurement. The supplied coverage does not provide a government response or resolve the discrepancy.

The jobs figures also get a closer reading. KNBS recorded 822,100 additional jobs in 2025 , excluding small-scale agriculture; 716,800, or 87.2 per cent, were informal. The Standard argues that counting new work is not the same as showing it is secure or well paid. That is an argument about the quality of employment, not a dispute over the reported totals.

On household finances, the article sets TIFA survey results against official inflation figures. In the June 2026 survey, 65 per cent said their circumstances had worsened since the 2022 election and 12 per cent said they had improved. KNBS reported October annual inflation of 7.3 per cent , with food and non-alcoholic beverages up 10.2 per cent and transport up 17.1 per cent. The piece uses those figures to explain why national claims of progress may feel distant from daily costs.

The first article also points to the Treasury’s projected Sh1.116 trillion deficit for 2026/27, asking the government to show what borrowing has achieved. It questions the appointment of former Meru governor Kiraitu Murungi to chair the National Oil Corporation board, asking what expertise was required and who else was considered. On civil liberties, it cites a 2024 court finding that the acting police chief was in contempt after repeatedly failing to appear over three men allegedly abducted by police, and says the Kenya National Commission on Human Rights documented further complaints about disappearances and excessive force around protests in 2026. The article ends mid-sentence, so its final point is unavailable.

The second piece is broader and less tied to individual figures. It says Ruto presented a Kenya of “remarkable achievement and rapid transformation”, then asks what progress means if it is not judged by whether people have rights, security and the chance to live with dignity. It invokes Article 132(1)(c) of the Constitution, but the supplied text ends before explaining the article’s full argument.

The two accounts agree on the central criticism but do not provide a full record of the President’s address or a response from State House. The harvest gap, the government’s evidence for its claims, and the outcomes of the named policy and appointment decisions remain untested in the material provided. The coverage is also thin: only The Standard is represented here, through two opinion pieces, so it cannot establish how other outlets assessed the address. No hearing, official reply or deadline is identified.
