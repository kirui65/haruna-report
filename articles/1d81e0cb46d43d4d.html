Saudi Arabia Reveals Jobs Kenyans Can Apply for Under New Labour Deal

Saudi Arabia&#039;s Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development has clarified that the technical annex signed with Kenya on November 12, 2026, applies exclusively to domestic workers and private drivers.

The ministry confirmed the agreement does not cover skilled professionals in healthcare, construction, education, or any other sector.

The Saudi government urged the public to depend solely on its official channels for recruitment information, a move widely interpreted as a response to what it regards as inflated characterisations of the deal&#039;s scope.

The annex was signed in Riyadh by Deputy Minister of Human Resources and Social Development for Labour Abdullah Abuthnain and Cabinet Secretary Alfred Nganga Mutua.

It builds on a bilateral domestic worker recruitment agreement the two countries concluded on July 5, 2026.

The annex rests on three pillars: lowering recruitment costs, raising the quality of domestic workers through training and qualification programmes, and protecting the rights of all parties in the contractual relationship.

A separate letter of intent also established a three-month pilot programme on voluntary recruitment fees, designed to keep Kenya competitive against other labour-sending countries.

During and after his Riyadh visit, Mutua announced that Saudi employment agencies had committed to 293,600 job opportunities for Kenyans spanning healthcare, construction, hospitality, education, transport, manufacturing, finance, oil and gas, and domestic work.

He also said both governments had agreed that their technical teams would conclude a separate skilled workers recruitment framework within one month.

Those announcements described intentions and future negotiations rather than the contents of the signed document.

The Saudi clarification, issued on November 17, draws a firm distinction between the binding domestic worker annex and the broader discussions on skilled labour that remain unresolved.

No specific salary rates or a revised minimum wage for Kenyan workers have been agreed upon, and the promised skilled workers agreement has not yet been signed.

Notably, the 293,600 job commitments were made by recruitment agencies and private companies, not incorporated into the annex.

Without a signed skilled workers agreement, the framework needed to channel Kenyans into those professional roles does not yet exist.

For Kenyans in domestic work and private driving, the annex represents a tangible improvement: streamlined recruitment procedures, reduced costs, and stronger contractual protections.

Pre-departure training programmes are also set to be strengthened to better prepare workers before they depart.

For those seeking professional roles, the Saudi government has confirmed no agreement is currently in place.

Kenyans who have been approached with offers of skilled employment in Saudi Arabia before a formal framework is concluded should treat such offers with caution.

Mutua separately noted that Kenya had engaged Saudi authorities regarding travel restrictions linked to reported Ebola cases, stating that the case had been identified and isolated with no community transmission recorded.

The Saudi ministry&#039;s statement did not address the travel restrictions.
