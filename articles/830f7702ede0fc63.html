Nyeri Catholic Archbishop threatens to sack officials who let politicians into the pulpit

Nyeri Catholic Archbishop Anthony Muheria has threatened to dismiss priests, catechists, coordinators and church secretaries who allow politicians to address congregations, issuing one of the starkest warnings yet from Kenya&#8217;s Catholic leadership as political pressure on places of worship intensifies ahead of the 10 August 2027 General Election.

Muheria delivered the directive as the Catholic Church escalates its campaign to keep politicians from converting Sunday services into campaign rallies — a practice that reached uncomfortable levels during the 2022 election cycle and shows every sign of repeating itself as Kenya enters another charged electoral season. With barely eight months to polling day, the pews have become some of the most coveted real estate in Kenyan politics, offering candidates direct access to organised, attentive and community-rooted audiences that no roadside rally can replicate.

The Archbishop was unambiguous about the consequences for those who facilitate such access. &#8220;Whoever allows a leader — anyone among you who allows anybody to speak in the church — will be fired immediately,&#8221; he declared. He extended the warning explicitly to administrative staff, making clear that arranging an opportunity for a politician to address worshippers was itself a dismissible offence. &#8220;If you are a coordinator, a secretary, and you are the one who arranged this thing, know that once it reaches me, you have lost your job and we will have an election to get another secretary,&#8221; he said.

The language was deliberate and pointed — the word &#8220;election&#8221; applied not to the national contest for public office but to the internal process of replacing a church official who had compromised the sanctity of the institution. It was a sharp piece of rhetorical symmetry, and it conveyed Muheria&#8217;s message with unmistakable clarity: the Church will govern itself by its own standards, regardless of the political weather outside its doors.

His frustration with the 2022 experience was evident. That election period saw politicians engage in heated and at times undignified confrontations inside churches, with congregations pressed into service as audiences for attacks on opponents and displays of political rivalry. Muheria cited those scenes as precisely what the current directive is designed to prevent. &#8220;Let us keep politics out of here, so that we can have respect in our churches,&#8221; he said.

He went further, drawing a sharp and deliberately colourful line between the spaces he considers appropriate for political contest and those he does not. &#8220;Let them go to bars, those dens. Let them go there into the fields — there is where they can fight, let them pull each other in the mud. But the church is God&#8217;s,&#8221; Muheria stated. The image was vivid and intentional: politics, in his framing, belongs to the rough and contested public square, not to the consecrated space where communities gather in a different spirit entirely.

Muheria&#8217;s directive follows a formal position adopted by the Kenya Conference of Catholic Bishops, which has barred politicians from delivering political addresses in churches across the country. The bishops argued that politicians have no shortage of alternative platforms — television, radio, public barazas, roadside rallies — through which to advance their agendas and seek support. What they do not have, the bishops maintained, is a right to the church&#8217;s platform, its congregation or its moral authority.

That authority is precisely what makes churches so attractive to Kenyan politicians. In a country where community trust in formal institutions has been eroded by years of broken promises and governance failures, the church retains a credibility that political parties and government agencies have largely forfeited. A politician who can stand before a congregation — or better still, be seen to receive the implicit endorsement of a priest or bishop — gains something that money and advertising cannot easily manufacture. The Catholic Church&#8217;s directive is, at its core, a refusal to allow that credibility to be borrowed for electoral purposes.

The timing carries additional significance. Kenya&#8217;s political landscape is shifting rapidly as the 2027 election approaches. Former Deputy President Rigathi Gachagua is consolidating opposition in the Mt Kenya region through his Democracy for the Citizens Party. The Linda Mwananchi Movement is expanding its national footprint. President William Ruto is defending his record against a cost-of-living narrative that has tested public patience. In that environment, every platform matters — and the Catholic Church, with its reach into rural and urban communities across the country, represents one of the most significant platforms of all.

By drawing its boundary now, eight months before polling day, the Church is sending a message that it intends to be a moral voice in the 2027 election rather than a political prop. Whether that boundary holds as campaign pressure mounts will be one of the quieter but consequential tests of institutional integrity in the months ahead.

The church, Muheria has made clear, is God&#8217;s. The mud is available elsewhere.
