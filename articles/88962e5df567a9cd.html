AI is making Kenyan students faster — but are they thinking better?

WhatsAppFacebookX

Generative tools are reshaping classrooms from Nairobi to rural TVET colleges, but educators warn that speed without judgement is not learning.

Artificial intelligence is changing how Kenyan students learn, but the education system&#8217;s most pressing challenge is ensuring that faster access to information translates into deeper understanding rather than a sophisticated shortcut to the same shallow thinking it has always struggled to address.

From secondary schools and universities to TVET institutions and professional training programmes, learners across Kenya can now use AI tools to summarise notes, explain difficult concepts, generate essay structures and produce written work in seconds. The opportunity is real and significant. So is the risk of mistaking the speed of an answer for the quality of the thinking behind it.

The question facing Kenya&#8217;s education institutions is no longer whether students will use AI. Many already do, quietly and routinely, in ways that range from entirely legitimate to academically dishonest. What matters now is whether they are being taught to use it with judgement — and whether the system is measuring the right things.

Verification must come first. Generative AI produces confident, well-structured responses that can be incomplete, outdated or factually wrong. A student researching Kenya&#8217;s economic policy, climate record, constitutional history or public health data cannot assume that a fluent paragraph is an accurate one. Learners need to compare sources, check publication dates, interrogate claims and develop an instinct for where information originates and who benefits from its framing.

This reality makes critical thinking more valuable, not less. For much of Kenya&#8217;s education history, students were rewarded for locating and reproducing information — the right answer in the right place at the right time. In an AI-enabled learning environment, the more important skill is assessing whether information is credible, relevant and applicable to a specific problem, and then doing something useful with it. That is a harder skill to teach and a harder one to examine, but it is the one that will determine how well a graduate functions in a workplace where AI is already embedded.

Privacy requires clearer and earlier guidance than most institutions are currently providing. Students should understand that personal data, confidential school records and sensitive information should not be entered into public AI platforms without consideration of where that data goes and how it is stored. As Kenya continues to build its data protection culture under the Data Protection Act and the oversight of the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner, responsible technology use must become part of digital literacy from an early age.

The plagiarism question demands a more considered institutional response than outright prohibition. Banning AI tools is difficult to enforce, often counterproductive and can obscure the larger pedagogical point. Education should measure whether a learner understands a subject, can defend a position under questioning and can apply knowledge to unfamiliar problems. That demands a greater emphasis on oral presentations, classroom discussions, practical assignments and project-based assessments — formats in which AI assistance is visible and the student&#8217;s own thinking is unavoidable.

Prompting is itself becoming a teachable and genuinely useful skill. A learner who can frame a precise question, provide sufficient context, refine an instruction and critically evaluate the response is developing capabilities that employers across sectors are beginning to actively seek. But prompting should sharpen thinking, not replace it. The student who accepts the first output without interrogation has not learned to use AI — they have learned to defer to it.

Parents and teachers carry responsibilities that go beyond policing tool use. The more productive questions are not whether a student used AI, but how they verified the answer, what they learned in the process, what they would challenge in the output and whether they can explain the idea without the tool in front of them. Those questions shift the focus from enforcement to education, which is where it belongs.

These capabilities will matter well beyond any examination hall. As AI integrates further into Kenya&#8217;s workplaces, employers will need people who can assess AI-generated information with scepticism, identify errors, exercise independent judgement and apply knowledge to problems that do not have pre-generated answers. Using AI fluently is useful. Knowing when to question its output is essential.

Kenya&#8217;s education system has navigated significant technology transitions before — computer studies, mobile learning, digital content, online classrooms. Each demanded adaptation. Each rewarded the institutions that treated the technology as a tool in service of learning rather than a substitute for it.

AI presents the same challenge at greater speed and scale. The institutions that respond best will be those that teach students not only how to access the technology, but how to question it, use it responsibly and combine it with the one thing no model can replicate: human judgement applied to real consequences.

AI can help Kenyan learners move faster. Education must still teach them how to think.
