Hackers Steal Kenyans' ID Numbers, Other Details from eCitizen, US Firm Reports

A cybercriminal group operating under the alias "ByteToBreach" has claimed responsibility for stealing approximately 740 gigabytes of data from Kenya&#039;s national eCitizen platform, an allegation that has spread widely across Kenyan technology publications since Monday but remains unconfirmed by any government authority.

The claim first appeared on an underground data-trading forum on the evening of November 22, 2026, and was subsequently flagged by VECERT, a Miami-based private threat intelligence firm that monitors such platforms.

As of Tuesday afternoon, no statement had been issued by the Ministry of ICT, the national cybersecurity response team KE-CIRT/CC, the National Treasury , or the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner.

According to VECERT&#039;s alert, ByteToBreach says it exfiltrated roughly 600GB of Know Your Customer (KYC) documents, which typically include copies of national identity cards and passports submitted by citizens, along with 100GB of government database records and 40GB of source code written in Erlang, Elixir, and Node.js.

Some files allegedly carried timestamps dating to November 14 and 15, 2026, though no data has been released publicly to support the claim.

The group alleges it gained initial access by exploiting a file-upload bypass vulnerability, which allowed it to deploy a webshell for remote code execution.

More critically, the post claims that eCitizen&#039;s OAuth and Single Sign-On (SSO) infrastructure was compromised.

The Social Health Authority, the Directorate of Criminal Investigations , Safaricom, and the National Police Service are named as potentially affected services due to their integration with eCitizen&#039;s identity layer, though no evidence has been produced showing those entities were directly accessed.

Cybersecurity analysts have flagged the alleged compromise of OAuth signing keys as particularly significant. In a federated identity system, eCitizen issues digitally signed tokens that connected services accept as proof of a user&#039;s identity.

Should those signing keys have been stolen, an attacker could theoretically forge tokens and impersonate citizens or officials across multiple platforms without ever needing their passwords, making a simple password change inadequate protection.

VECERT recommended that technical teams rotate JWT and OAuth signing keys immediately, revoke all active tokens, and audit file-upload endpoints for webshells.

ByteToBreach is a financially motivated group believed to be linked to Algeria and active since at least June 2025.

Romanian and Hungarian authorities have tied the group to attacks on government systems, including Romania&#039;s national land registry, in July 2026. However, its claims have not always held up to scrutiny.

In November 2026, the group claimed to have stolen 70GB from NSW Health in Australia; police there assessed the actual volume at around 4GB and said no patient data was involved, with an officer describing the actor as "a nuisance" with a history of false claims.

The alleged breach follows a documented increase in attacks on Kenyan government infrastructure. The Communications Authority of Kenya&#039;s report covering July to September 2026 recorded approximately 2.696 billion cyber threat events, a 14.45% rise from the preceding quarter, with web application attacks up 57.62% and brute-force attacks up 85.41%.

Under Kenya&#039;s Data Protection Act 2019, data controllers must notify the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner within 72 hours of confirming a breach involving personal data. No such notification has been made public.
