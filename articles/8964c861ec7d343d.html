Murang’a determined to reduce child stunting to below five percent

WhatsAppFacebookX

Murang’a County has set an ambitious target of reducing childhood stunting to below five per cent by 2030 as it rolls out a five-year nutrition action plan aimed at tackling all forms of malnutrition and improving household feeding practices.

The Second County Nutrition Action Plan (CNAP II), covering the period 2026–2031 seeks to address stunting, wasting, underweight, overweight, obesity and micronutrient deficiencies through coordinated interventions involving health, agriculture, education, water, trade, gender and social services.

Speaking during the launch at the County Headquarters on Wednesday, Deputy Governor Stephen Munania stated that the strategy would strengthen preventive healthcare while integrating nutrition into the county&#8217;s agricultural and education programmes.

He singled out the expanded school feeding programme which will commence in January 2027 and cover public primary schools, Junior Schools and Early Childhood Development (ECD) centres.

The programme, he noted, will provide meals at Sh15 per plate, with the county targeting more than 250,000 plates daily to improve learners’ nutrition, school attendance and concentration.

Munania added that the initiative would also create a reliable market for local farmers by sourcing food supplies, including vegetables, mangoes and milk, within the county.

He noted that linking school feeding to local agricultural production would improve farmers&#8217; incomes while ensuring learners access nutritious meals.

“We want to create an economy around food, an economy around nutrition and an economy around education while ensuring our farmers benefit more,” he remarked.

The County Nutrition Coordinator Nancy Mwangi explained the new plan builds on the first County Nutrition Action Plan, implemented between 2020 and 2025, which contributed to reducing childhood stunting from 19.2 per cent in 2014 to 10 per cent.

She said the county was targeting to reduce the rate to below five per cent by 2030 through improved maternal and child nutrition, nutrition education and increased access to diverse and balanced diets.

However, she noted that nutritional challenges persisted among different population groups, with 43 per cent of adolescent boys being too thin for their age, compared to 12 percent of girls.

She added that 27 per cent of women were overweight or obese, while 17 per cent of men were underweight. Among children, six per cent were underweight, while ten per cent were experiencing stunting.

Mwangi urged households to improve their feeding habits by ensuring daily meals comprised at least five of the ten recognized food groups, noting that dietary diversity was essential in preventing malnutrition and related illnesses.

“Food is medicine. If we don&#8217;t take food as medicine, we will take medicine as food,” she stated.

The nutritionist added that the county would intensify nutrition education at the grassroots to equip families with knowledge on locally available nutritious foods, food preparation and balanced diets.

Meanwhile, Nutrition International representative Dr Elijah Mbiti observed that inadequate nutrition knowledge and poor household food choices continued to undermine efforts to address malnutrition, even in communities with sufficient food production.

Mbiti noted that some households prioritized selling nutritious farm produce without setting aside enough for their children’s dietary needs.

“Some households produce eggs but sell all of them, yet the children in those homes also need these eggs for their nutritional needs,” he said, urging families to balance income generation with household nutritional requirements.

The five-year plan brings together county departments, development partners and communities in a coordinated effort to improve nutrition outcomes through better access to nutritious foods, strengthened healthcare services and increased public awareness.

The strategy places emphasis on prevention, recognizing that tackling malnutrition requires more than adequate food production and must also involve better feeding practices, household awareness and access to essential nutrition services.
