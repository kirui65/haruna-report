Senegal reinstated as Afcon 2025 champions by CAS ruling

WhatsAppFacebookX

Court of Arbitration for Sport unanimously overturns CAF&#8217;s controversial decision to award Morocco the title after the chaotic Casablanca final

Senegal have been reinstated as Africa Cup of Nations 2025 champions after the Court of Arbitration for Sport unanimously upheld their appeal against the Confederation of African Football&#8217;s decision to strip them of the title and award it to Morocco following one of the most chaotic finals in the tournament&#8217;s history.

The Swiss-based sports court announced its verdict on Tuesday, confirming that Senegal&#8217;s original 1-0 victory after extra time stands, and that the title belongs to the Lions of Teranga — not the Atlas Lions. &#8220;The Court of Arbitration for Sport has upheld the appeal by the Senegalese Football Federation against the Confederation of African Football and the Royal Moroccan Football Federation concerning the final of the Africa Cup of Nations Morocco 2025,&#8221; CAS said in a formal statement. The decision was unanimous.

To understand why this ruling matters, it is necessary to revisit what happened in Casablanca in January. With the final still goalless and tensions running high, the referee awarded Morocco a penalty. Senegal&#8217;s players, convinced the decision was unjust and incensed by what they believed was home-nation bias, walked off the pitch in protest. It was a dramatic, ill-disciplined moment — but a temporary one. The players returned. The match restarted. Senegal won 1-0 after extra time and celebrated what they believed was a historic continental triumph.

Then CAF intervened. In March, the confederation&#8217;s Appeals Committee overturned the result entirely, awarding Morocco a 3-0 victory and the title, citing Articles 82 and 84 of the AFCON Regulations. Those articles state that if a team refuses to play or leaves the ground before the regular end of the match without the referee&#8217;s authorisation, it shall be considered the loser and eliminated from the competition. It was a seismic decision — one that stripped a champion of its crown not on the pitch but in a committee room, and one that immediately drew fierce criticism from across the African football community.

Senegal&#8217;s federation went straight to CAS. Their argument was straightforward and, as it has now been confirmed, legally sound: the Senegalese players did not abandon the match. They returned to the pitch. The referee restarted the final. The game was completed. The result was produced on the field of play under the oversight of the match official. If the referee had determined that the conditions for resuming the match had not been met, the game would not have restarted. It did restart. That, the CAS panel ruled, was determinative.

The panel found that the referee&#8217;s decision to restart the final demonstrated that &#8220;the conditions for resuming the match had been met,&#8221; and therefore that Articles 82 and 84 were &#8220;not applicable in this case.&#8221; CAF had, in effect, applied rules designed for teams that abandon matches to a team that completed one. CAS corrected that error with a clarity that left no room for ambiguity.

The ruling has implications that extend beyond the two nations directly involved. It raises serious questions about CAF&#8217;s governance and its decision-making processes in high-stakes situations. The confederation&#8217;s March ruling was always legally fragile — the basic facts of the final, that the match was completed and a result was produced, made the application of abandonment regulations extraordinarily difficult to justify. That CAF pressed ahead regardless, in a decision that awarded the title to the host nation, was always going to face the most intense scrutiny at an independent tribunal.

For Senegal, the ruling restores not just a trophy but a sense of justice — and of narrative. The Lions of Teranga, who have long been among Africa&#8217;s most admired footballing nations, now hold the continental title they won on the pitch in January without qualification or asterisk. For their players, their federation and their supporters, the past several months have been defined by an injustice that is now formally corrected.

For Morocco, it is a painful reversal. The Atlas Lions had celebrated a home triumph. That celebration has now been officially undone.

African football&#8217;s governing body will need to do considerably more than accept the CAS ruling — it will need to explain how it reached a decision that a unanimous international panel has found to be wrong.
