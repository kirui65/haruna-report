Kenyans to Start Paying Toll Fees on Thika Highway, 4 Other Busy Roads

Kenya&#039;s National Treasury has placed road tolling at the heart of its infrastructure financing strategy, releasing a pipeline of highway projects worth hundreds of billions of shillings that would transfer construction and maintenance costs from the government to private investors and motorists.

The proposals are contained in the 2027 Budget Policy Statement (BPS) and represent the government&#039;s most decisive shift yet towards a user-pay funding model for the country&#039;s busiest transport corridors, at a time when public debt servicing alone is projected to reach KSh 1.918 trillion in the 2027/28 financial year.

The most consequential project is the upgrade of the 461-kilometre Nairobi–Mombasa highway (A8), which the government intends to widen from two lanes to at least four and convert into an access-controlled toll road.

A previous proposal valued at KSh 468 billion, submitted by US investor Everstrong Capital, was rejected by the PPP Committee in July 2025 for failing to meet statutory requirements.

The revived plan has since moved to the transaction-adviser stage, with bid evaluation under way following a fresh expression of interest published in August 2026.

The Nairobi–Thika–Juja–Kenol–Sagana–Marua highway is listed as a KSh 100 billion PPP with a proposed 30-year concession period, targeting financial close in the 2028/29 financial year.

The Treasury has classified it as a user-pay investment without government support, meaning the private party absorbs all commercial and demand risk with no contingent liability falling on the Exchequer.

In western Kenya , the 243-kilometre Mau Summit–Eldoret–Malaba corridor, a section of the Northern Corridor carrying close to 3,000 trucks daily and over 35 million tonnes of cargo annually, is earmarked for upgrade to a four-lane toll road at an estimated KSh 130 billion, with construction pencilled in for June 2027 and financing expected from the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank.

Two bypass concessions round out the pipeline. The Nairobi Southern Bypass is set for a KSh 12.265 billion, 25-year operation and tolling concession, while the proposed 38.6-kilometre Mombasa Northern Bypass carries a KSh 30 billion price tag over 30 years, blending private capital with government viability-gap funding.

A High Court ruling in November 2026 has added complexity to the programme. The court declared portions of the PPP Act, 2021 unconstitutional insofar as they permit projects creating public debt or guarantees to bypass Parliament.

Pure user-pay toll roads without state backing can still proceed under committee approval, but any arrangement involving annuity payments or sovereign guarantees must now secure parliamentary consent.

The tolling agenda has also met organised resistance from consumer groups. The Motorists Association of Kenya (MAK) has rejected the proposed 30-year concession for the Rironi–Mau Summit highway , advocating instead for a temporary fuel levy of KSh 5 to KSh 10 per litre.

The Consumer Federation of Kenya challenged a proposed KSh 8-per-kilometre toll on the Nairobi–Nakuru–Mau Summit corridor in court, arguing it would worsen the cost of living, though both petitions were dismissed.

None of the major projects has yet reached financial close, and toll rates remain undisclosed across the board.

With the Nairobi–Thika and bypass deals targeting 2028/29 for financial close, the entire programme will face continuity scrutiny in the period following Kenya&#039;s August 2027 General Election.
