Not safe: New study raises alarm over findings on chicken meat sold in Nairobi

Almost all raw chicken meat sold at butcheries and non-supermarket retail outlets across Nairobi County is contaminated with harmful bacteria, according to a new study by the Kenya Veterinary Association (KVA).

The study, conducted by the KVA Technical Working Group on Processing, Food Security and Safety, and Quality Assurance in collaboration with the Department of Public Health, Pharmacology and Toxicology at the University of Nairobi , assessed 264 retail establishments.

Researchers collected chicken meat samples and tested them for three pathogens while also interviewing vendors about their storage, handling, and hygiene practices.

Escherichia coli was detected in 94.7% of samples. Staphylococcus aureus was present in 39.3% of samples, and Salmonella in 8.1%. Co-contamination with both E. coli and S. aureus was recorded in 34% of cases.

The hygiene data collected from the outlets helps explain the scale of the problem.

Some 92.8% of establishments used wooden chopping boards, which are porous and resistant to thorough cleaning.

Seven in ten outlets had no handwashing facility, and 54.6% stored chicken at room temperature rather than under refrigeration.

Surfaces with visible meat and blood residue were observed in 75% of establishments, while only 9.9% of attendants were seen wearing clean protective clothing.

Most chicken arrived at retail points fresh and warm, with 92.4% of outlets receiving unchilled meat. Around 79.2% of outlets sourced stock from distributors or wholesalers, and 47% sourced from local farms.

The study also identified significant antimicrobial resistance among the bacteria isolated. Some 44.7% of E. coli isolates and 28.4% of S. aureus isolates were classified as multidrug-resistant, meaning they resisted three or more classes of antibiotics.

E. coli showed high resistance to cotrimoxazole at 65.9% and ampicillin at 50.8%. Salmonella isolates displayed low resistance levels overall, with 99.6% classified as non-multidrug-resistant.

Statistical analysis identified specific practices linked to higher contamination risk. Chicken handled on shared surfaces carried more than three times the odds of E. coli contamination compared with meat handled on dedicated surfaces.

The use of wooden chopping boards was associated with more than three times higher odds of S. aureus contamination.

Meat sourced through middlemen faced higher odds of Salmonella contamination, while freezer storage was linked to 72% lower odds of Salmonella presence.

Attendants with college or university education were associated with significantly lower odds of contamination across all three pathogens.

KVA National President Kelvin Osore said the findings highlight an urgent need to strengthen food safety standards across the entire poultry supply chain.

The KVA is recommending that the government introduce mandatory food safety training for meat handlers, enforce cold-chain standards, and conduct routine microbial surveillance.

Retailers are urged to replace wooden boards with stainless steel or food-grade plastic surfaces and ensure functioning handwashing stations with soap and clean water.

County governments are advised to develop localised food safety policies and strengthen inspection services, while consumers are encouraged to demand safe food and practise proper handling at home.
