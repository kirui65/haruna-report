Ranking top in interviews alone won’t guarantee job, court rules in Maseno VC case

WhatsAppFacebookX

Ranking highest in a job interview does not guarantee automatic selection for a public position, according to a Kisumu court.

Ranking highest in a job interview does not guarantee automatic selection for a public position, a Kisumu court has ruled, in a decision that has brought into focus the delicate balance between merit, gender equality and regional representation in public appointments.

In a ruling, Justice Joe Omido dismissed two consolidated petitions challenging the Public Service Commission’s (PSC) decision to forward the names of candidates who finished first, fourth and fifth in the Maseno University Vice-Chancellor interviews, leaving out those who came second and third.

The petitioners, Evans Ochieng Aseto and Solomon Erick Omondi Okello, questioned why Prof Bernard O Aduda and Prof Francis Jakim Mulaa, who emerged second and third respectively, were overlooked in favour of Prof Kimani Chege Gabriel and Prof Mary Jepkemoi Kipsat, who finished fourth and fifth.

The two petitions were consolidated, with Mr Aseto and Mr Okello becoming the first and second petitioners respectively. The Public Service Commission, the Council of Maseno University, the Ministry of Education and the Attorney General were named as the first, second, third and fourth respondents.

The petitioners argued that the decision departed from the published interview rankings without any convincing explanation, raising questions about fairness, transparency and the place of merit in public appointments.

However, Justice Omido held that securing a higher interview score does not, on its own, create an automatic right to be recommended for appointment. He said the commission was also required to consider other constitutional values, including gender balance and diversity.

The dispute arose from a recruitment process initiated by the PSC on December 16, 2025, when it advertised the position of Vice-Chancellor at Maseno University, inviting applications from suitably qualified candidates.

The advertisement outlined the academic qualifications, professional experience, leadership skills and administrative credentials expected of applicants seeking to head the public university. Ten candidates were shortlisted, with interviews conducted on April 14 and 16, 2026.

When the results were released, Prof Bulitia Godrick Mathews emerged top with 79 marks, followed by Prof Aduda with 77.5 marks and Prof Mulaa with 77 marks.

Prof Chege came fourth with 76 marks, while Prof Kipsat secured fifth position with 74 marks, tying with Prof Joseph Ouma Rasowo.

Prof Stephen O. Odebero scored 71.5 marks, while Prof Godfrey S. Mise managed 67 marks. Prof Eric Otieno Nyamitha was listed as “Not Ranked”, while Prof Charles O. A. Omwadho declined to attend the interview.

However, when the PSC forwarded its recommendations to the Maseno University Council, the list comprised Prof Mathews, Prof Chege and Prof Kipsat.

The decision effectively shut out Prof Aduda and Prof Mulaa despite their strong performances at the interviews, while opening the door for candidates who had finished behind them.

That became the central point of the legal battle, with the petitioners arguing that the commission could not publish interview scores, rank candidates and subsequently disregard the order without providing a clear explanation.

They maintained that the unexplained selection had turned an otherwise competitive recruitment exercise into an arbitrary process, undermining the constitutional principles of fairness, accountability, transparency and equal treatment.

The petitioners also questioned the treatment of Prof Nyamitha, the substantive Deputy Vice-Chancellor in charge of Academic Affairs and Acting Vice-Chancellor at the time. Despite being shortlisted and interviewed, he was classified as “Not Ranked”, a decision the petitioners considered discriminatory and inconsistent with the principles of fair administrative action.

They argued that the PSC had failed to provide reasons or disclose objective criteria justifying its decision to exclude the second and third-ranked candidates while recommending those who finished fourth and fifth.

The petitioners further contended that the failure to explain the departure from the published order of merit rendered the process arbitrary, discriminatory, irrational and procedurally unfair.

They maintained that, having published the interview scores and rankings, the commission was under an obligation to uphold the principle of merit and explain any departure from the results.

On Prof Nyamitha’s exclusion from the rankings, they argued that the decision was discriminatory and contrary to the principles of fair administrative action, particularly given his position as the substantive Deputy Vice-Chancellor in charge of Academic Affairs and Acting Vice-Chancellor.

Among the reliefs they sought was a declaration that recommending candidates who ranked first, fourth and fifth while excluding those who came second and third, without lawful justification, was unconstitutional, illegal, irrational and procedurally unfair.

They also sought orders quashing the recruitment process and the recommendations made by the PSC, as well as an order of mandamus compelling the commission to commence a fresh recruitment exercise in accordance with the Constitution and applicable laws.

Further, they wanted the court to restrain the university council from appointing anyone as Vice-Chancellor through the contested recruitment process. They also sought an order requiring any future recruitment exercise to comply with the Constitution and the law.

The PSC, however, defended its decision, arguing that Prof Chege, who ranked fourth, was recommended on the basis of ethnic balance, while Prof Kipsat, who finished fifth, was selected on gender balance considerations.

Regarding Prof Nyamitha’s classification as “Not Ranked”, the commission said that although he had been interviewed, he did not meet the requirement of having served for at least five years as a Deputy Vice-Chancellor or Principal of a constituent college.

While the petitioners insisted that the selection violated the Constitution, Justice Omido maintained that the law did not create an automatic entitlement to recommendation based solely on numerical interview rankings.

“The Petitioner’s dissatisfaction with the outcome of the recruitment exercise, and particularly with the fact that candidates who obtained higher interview scores were not all forwarded to the University Council, cannot by itself justify the intervention sought. The Constitution does not establish an automatic entitlement to recommendation based solely on numerical interview ranking,” said Mr Omido.

The judge said the evidence demonstrated that the commission had considered candidates’ interview performance alongside other considerations falling within the constitutional values governing public service appointments.

“The Court has found no sufficient basis for setting aside that assessment. The Commission’s consideration of diversity, gender representation and other constitutional values was not a departure from the law; it was part of its duty to apply the law. I therefore find that the petitioner has not established a violation of Article 27 or a breach of any legitimate expectation capable of invalidating the recruitment process,” said Mr Omido.

The court ultimately found that the petitioners had failed to establish sufficient grounds for granting the constitutional and judicial review reliefs they had sought.
