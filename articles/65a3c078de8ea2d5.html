Gachagua bids for Somali vote with corruption accountability pledge

WhatsAppFacebookX

The DCP leader promises North Eastern Kenya a reckoning with its political class — and a seat at the table of the sixth administration

Former Deputy President Rigathi Gachagua has made a direct pitch to the Somali community and North Eastern Kenya, promising that a future administration under his leadership would pursue accountability for public funds mismanaged in the region and end what he described as decades of political exploitation by local leaders.

In a statement directed at residents of Eastleigh, Kamukunji and the broader Somali community across the country, Gachagua was blunt about the scale of the grievance. &#8220;The Somali community in Kenya — a community of hardworking and astute businessmen and women — has been taken for granted by its leaders for far too long,&#8221; he said. &#8220;With over a trillion shillings disbursed to the North Eastern region since 2013, the only thing this community has received is incitement from busybodies in Nairobi, and looters of their resources building skyscrapers in Nairobi at the expense of residents in dire need of education, security, healthcare and other social amenities.&#8221;

The statement, which drew an enthusiastic response in Eastleigh, signals a deliberate and significant widening of Gachagua&#8217;s political coalition beyond the Mt Kenya base that remains his electoral heartland. Kamukunji MP Yusuf Hassan has already quit the Jubilee Party for Gachagua&#8217;s Democracy for the Citizens Party, a move analysts say could reshape political alignments in Nairobi ahead of 2027.

The arithmetic behind the outreach is straightforward. North Eastern Kenya lacks a dominant opposition political figure capable of rallying voters against the President — a gap the opposition, and Gachagua in particular, is actively seeking to exploit. The region&#8217;s Somali community, concentrated in Eastleigh and across Garissa, Wajir, Mandera and Isiolo counties, votes with notable cohesion when its leadership unites behind a candidate. For Gachagua&#8217;s sixth administration project, that cohesion could be transformative.

His pitch is built on a corruption accountability narrative that has been gaining unexpected traction in the north. Analysis of Gachagua&#8217;s political messaging suggests his corruption narrative is striking a chord in Northern Kenya, where voters are increasingly described as desperate to be freed from what observers characterise as the region&#8217;s entrenched political elite.

The government&#8217;s response has been equally pointed. Health Cabinet Secretary Aden Duale, speaking from Eastleigh, accused Gachagua of insulting the business community there, and rallied support for President William Ruto&#8217;s re-election, arguing that North Eastern Kenya and pastoralist counties would mobilise overwhelming support for the President. Duale specifically credited Ruto with resolving the community&#8217;s decades-long difficulties in accessing identity cards, passports and birth certificates — a genuine administrative grievance that has lingered since independence.

Opposition to Gachagua&#8217;s North Eastern push has taken pointed forms — a Mandera deputy governor compared supporting the DCP leader to eating pork, using a religious framing explicitly designed to discourage Muslim voters from backing him. Such interventions underscore the depth of establishment anxiety about what Gachagua&#8217;s coalition might become if the outreach succeeds.

Gachagua&#8217;s position on the Somali community has not always been uncomplicated. Earlier controversies around remarks about appointments and academic admissions prompted accusations that hardened some community leaders against him. He subsequently moved to distance himself from those characterisations, stating: &#8220;I have tremendous respect for that community; they are very honourable people. I do business with people from the Somali community, and their word is like a banker&#8217;s cheque.&#8221;

The current outreach appears designed to consolidate that reset and move decisively past earlier friction. By framing the issue as one of accountability — not identity — Gachagua is attempting to position himself as the candidate who will pursue corrupt local leaders rather than the community they represent. It is a distinction with real political potential in a region where frustration with its own political class runs genuinely deep.

Whether the Somali community and North Eastern Kenya ultimately move towards Gachagua in August 2027 will depend on factors beyond any single statement or visit. Local governors and MPs retain considerable influence over how their constituents vote, and most remain aligned with President Ruto. But the conversation has shifted. A year ago, a credible opposition pitch to North Eastern Kenya was barely visible. Today, it is one of the more consequential subplots of Kenya&#8217;s most closely watched election in a generation.
