Zambia HIV Agency Warns Donor Cuts Threaten Treatment Gains

WhatsAppFacebookX

Zambia&#x27;s National AIDS Council warns shrinking donor funding threatens its 98-98-97 HIV gains after UNAIDS data showed PrEP uptake fell by half.

Zambia's National HIV/AIDS/STI/TB Council (NAC) urged health authorities and international partners on November 25, 2026, to safeguard equitable access to HIV prevention, testing, and treatment, warning that shrinking foreign donor support threatens decades of public health progress.

NAC Director General Dr. Kebby Musokotwane issued the warning ahead of World AIDS Day on December 1 under the global theme "Ending AIDS Starts With Access," one day after UNAIDS reported that pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) uptake in Zambia fell by more than half between 2024 and 2025.

On treatment metrics, Zambia remains one of the strongest performers globally. Ministry of Health and NAC figures show the country has surpassed the UNAIDS 95-95-95 adult benchmarks, reaching 98-98-97: 98 percent of people living with HIV know their status, 98 percent of those diagnosed receive antiretroviral therapy, and 97 percent on treatment achieve viral suppression. UNAIDS estimates also show new infections fell 52 percent between 2010 and 2025.

Yet prevention data reveal a sharp divergence beneath that headline success. According to Ministry of Health figures cited during national prevention planning, Zambia recorded roughly 30,000 new HIV infections in 2024, up from 23,000 in 2023. Adolescents and young people aged 15 to 24 accounted for 38 percent of new cases, with adolescent girls and young women facing three times the risk of male peers.

Zambia's vulnerability stems heavily from how its HIV response has been financed. A peer-reviewed modeling analysis co-authored by Musokotwane and published in the Annals of Internal Medicine noted that the United States President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) supplied 84 percent of Zambia's HIV program funding at the start of 2025, making the country acutely exposed to bilateral aid disruptions.

Global financing data released by UNAIDS on November 24 underscore the scale of that shock. Across low- and middle-income countries, international HIV resources dropped by more than $1 billion in 2025, leaving a $4.3 billion annual shortfall against what is required to end AIDS as a public health threat by 2030. In Zambia, Cameroon, and Nigeria, PrEP delivery dropped by over 50 percent.

Bilateral replacement funding has also stalled. According to Health Policy Watch, Zambia postponed signing a proposed multiyear United States health aid memorandum of understanding in late 2025 and requested revisions in February 2026 after Washington linked the billion-dollar package to access to Zambian minerals, including copper and cobalt.

Musokotwane told local media on November 25 that service access remains the dividing line between life and death, particularly for low-income households, young people, and rural residents who face long travel distances to clinics. Earlier Zambian warnings at the June 2026 United Nations High-Level Meeting on HIV/AIDS cautioned that funding gaps risk drug resistance, premature deaths, and lost productivity.

UNAIDS data indicate that community-led clinics have absorbed the sharpest blow from financing cuts. A 2026 UNAIDS survey of 79 community organizations across 47 countries documented a 50 percent drop in PrEP provision, alongside an 85 percent decline in services for gay and bisexual men and an 82 percent drop for sex workers. In Zambia, the Civil Society Self-Coordinating Mechanism has urged Lusaka to adopt a national social contracting policy so community groups can maintain outreach as foreign grants recede.

To close the prevention gap, Zambia launched twice-yearly injectable lenacapavir in December 2025 alongside its 2025-2030 HIV Prevention Roadmap, which aims to halve annual new infections to 15,000 by 2030, and introduced the dapivirine vaginal ring in April 2026. The strategy relies on integrating HIV services into primary healthcare and tapping domestic budgets, including the Constituency Development Fund, to offset donor cuts.

Yet rolling out long-acting prevention products requires sustained procurement budgets and clinic staffing at the exact moment external grants are contracting. Whether Zambia can protect its 98-98-97 treatment gains while reversing the 2024-2025 drop in PrEP coverage will depend on domestic budget allocations and any revised donor agreements ahead of World AIDS Day.
