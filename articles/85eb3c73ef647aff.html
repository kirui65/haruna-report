Sifuna opens Linda Mwananchi Movement’s first US office

WhatsAppFacebookX

The Hackettstown office anchors a US tour with meetings planned in six states, but the reports offer no attendance or membership figures.

Nairobi Senator Edwin Sifuna has opened the Linda Mwananchi Movement’s first international office in Hackettstown, New Jersey, giving the group a formal base for its US diaspora activities ahead of the 2027 General Election.

The opening took place on Saturday, November 21 , as Sifuna led a delegation on a tour of the United States. The movement announced the start of its activities in a message on X. Sifuna said the group was “beginning work officially” and opening its first international office.

People Daily’s two reports trace the shift from plan to event: its Saturday article said the office was due to open at noon; a report published Sunday said it had opened. The coverage describes this as the movement’s first formal international office, but does not give the address beyond Hackettstown or identify who will run it.

The office opening is part of a wider programme of meetings with Kenyans living in the US. Sifuna released the itinerary on November 17 , with stops planned in Pennsylvania, Maryland, Washington DC, Texas, Washington State and California.

The schedule includes town halls, church services, dinners with business communities and meetings with diaspora groups. The next listed engagement after New Jersey was a church service and town hall in Allentown, Pennsylvania, on Sunday, November 22. The delegation was also due to meet communities in Maryland and Washington DC before travelling to Dallas and Houston.

Further activities were planned for Seattle and California, with the tour expected to end with a dinner in Silicon Valley on December 7 . Sifuna acknowledged that the delegation would not reach every city or state and told Kenyans in the US to meet the team where they could.

People Daily presents the office as a base for coordinating diaspora engagement and extending the movement’s organisation beyond Kenya. The stated purpose of the tour is to hear concerns and present the movement’s political agenda. The reports do not describe specific policy proposals or say what the office’s day-to-day operations will involve.

Both accounts connect the opening to political organising ahead of the 2027 election. They describe the office and tour as ways to build relationships with Kenyans abroad, but neither provides evidence of how many people attended the opening or the planned events, nor any indication of the movement’s level of support among diaspora communities.

There is also little independent detail in the coverage: both reports are from the same outlet and rely on the movement’s announcement and Sifuna’s itinerary. The development has had limited outlet coverage in the reports provided, so its wider reception cannot be assessed here.

The open question is whether a US office and a run of diaspora meetings will lead to lasting organisation or political support, either among Kenyans abroad or at home. The reports do not give membership figures, funding details or a measure of what success would look like for the initiative.
