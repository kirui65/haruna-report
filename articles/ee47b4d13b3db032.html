Nikki Germany: When big tech restructures, where does Kenya’s tech talent go next?

WhatsAppFacebookX

Microsoft's reported decision to close its Africa Development Centre (ADC) in Nairobi, seven years after its establishment, has raised fresh questions about Kenya's future as a technology hub. Employees were reportedly informed of the closure on October 12. However, Microsoft has described the changes as part of a global restructuring affecting its engineering operations in Kenya, without confirming a complete shutdown.

For the engineers and other professionals whose livelihoods are affected, this is more than a corporate restructuring. It represents years of experience, professional relationships and work on products used around the world. Their contribution to Kenya’s technology ecosystem should not be overlooked, and neither should the uncertainty facing them and their families. As an industry, we have a responsibility to ensure that their experience is not lost.

Kenya is often described as Africa’s Silicon Savannah, and developments of this nature inevitably raise questions about what that reputation rests on. At Moringa, where we train thousands of young Kenyans for technology careers, we see a bigger question beyond the job losses: has Kenya built an ecosystem and a talent base strong enough to retain and develop world-class engineers when multinationals change direction? Education is central to the answer, because the talent that stays relevant through such shifts is shaped long before a first job.

The ADC’s scale shows why. Announced in 2019 with sites in Nairobi and Lagos, it came with a pledge of $100 million over five years and 500 engineers across both centres by 2023. Nairobi alone had more than 450 full-time employees by March 2022. Kenyan engineers worked in international teams on products built for global markets, and that experience is the investment’s most valuable legacy. Restructuring may eliminate jobs, but it does not erase skills.

It also reminds us that announcements are not outcomes. Microsoft closed its Lagos centre in 2024. That same year, it and UAE-based G42 announced a $1 billion digital investment initiative in Kenya, centred on a proposed geothermal-powered data centre at Olkaria. The project has since stalled, reportedly over power capacity and payment guarantees. None of this means Kenya is losing its appeal, but it exposes the risk of depending on decisions made beyond our borders.

So the real test of Silicon Savannah is what happens when investment changes direction. Can experienced professionals find new opportunities locally, strengthen Kenyan businesses, or build the companies that create the next generation of jobs? Financial services, fintech, telecoms and emerging technology firms can absorb many of them, but salary gaps and fierce competition for global remote roles mean this will not happen automatically. Local employers must offer strong engineering cultures, real career progression and the chance to build competitive products.

That is why education must move beyond job readiness to career resilience. Teaching the technologies employers want today is no longer enough when AI is changing how work is done. At Moringa, we focus on strong technical foundations, so learners understand how software, data, and systems work rather than only today's framework; on problem-solving and product thinking; and on the habit of continuous learning that keeps professionals relevant as employer needs evolve.

Our research shows why. In Moringa’s Graduate Impact Study 2026, 70.9% of surveyed alumni who secured opportunities did so before graduation or within six months. Yet all 14 employers surveyed named technical execution a strength of our graduates, while 63.4% said communication needed to improve. Technical skill gets people hired; communication, business understanding and continuous learning keep them relevant. Our Workforce Capability Report 2026 found that 77% of organisations use online learning platforms, but only 16% rated their upskilling as very effective.

Each partner has a practical role. Employers should offer structured internships and apprenticeships, which accounted for 29.1% of our alumni first opportunities, with real mentorship. They should build technical career ladders that do not force engineers into management, and hire experienced professionals released by restructurings to lead products and mentor juniors.

Education providers should co-design curricula with employers and review them regularly, build learning around real Kenyan problems, teach communication and commercial awareness alongside technical depth, publish graduate outcomes openly, and offer retooling pathways in areas such as AI and cybersecurity for experienced professionals.

Government can make it easier to build and scale from Kenya through predictable tax and regulation, reliable and affordable power, and procurement that gives local technology firms a fair chance. It can reward employers who take on interns, and help founders with early-stage capital and easier cross-border payments, so engineers leaving multinationals can start companies serving global markets from Nairobi.

Microsoft's restructuring is not proof that Silicon Savannah has lost its place in the global technology economy. It is an opportunity to rethink what lasting investment should achieve. The true measure of Kenya's success will not be how many global companies open offices here, but how much local capability remains, grows, and creates new opportunities when they change direction.
