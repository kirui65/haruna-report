MozzartBet, BetPawa, 1XBet Left Out As GRA Names 11 Betting Firms Cleared To Operate In Kenya

WhatsAppFacebookX

Kenya’s multibillion-shilling betting industry is facing fresh regulatory scrutiny after the Gambling Regulatory Authority (GRA) issued a list of companies cleared to operate during the 2026/2027 financial year, conspicuously leaving out several popular betting brands, including MozzartBet, BetPawa, 1xBet and 22Bet.

In a letter dated November 2, 2026, addressed to Safaricom Chief Executive Officer Peter Ndegwa and copied to Steve Chege, the regulator identified 11 companies it said had satisfied the Board’s licensing requirements and received relevant licences for the 2026/2027 financial year.

The communication, referenced GRA 15/1 VOL.II (19), is particularly significant because it directs Safaricom to enforce the licensing requirements by restricting payment channels associated with operators that have not secured the necessary authorisation.

The conspicuous absence of MozzartBet is especially noteworthy given the company’s established presence in Kenya’s betting market and its previous encounters with government authorities over licensing and financial transactions.

Other familiar names missing from the November 2 list include BetPawa, 1xBet, 22Bet, HelaBet, KessBet, Pakakumi, Radabet and Shabiki.

The omission raises serious questions about the licensing status of companies that have continued to enjoy visibility among Kenyan punters, attract deposits and compete for customers in one of Africa’s most active betting markets.

Importantly, the omission establishes that these brands were not included in the GRA’s published list for the 2026/2027 financial year as at November 2. It does not, without further confirmation from the regulator, establish that every company has been permanently denied a licence or is necessarily operating illegally.

There are also questions about the completeness of the regulator’s own published information.

Although the letter describes all 11 companies as having met licensing requirements, the entries for Wezabet and Betnare contain dashes instead of licence numbers and PayBill numbers.

Gamemania’s entry similarly identifies a public gaming licence number without displaying a corresponding bookmaker licence number.

This raises an important transparency question: how should Safaricom, customers and other enforcement agencies independently verify the precise categories of licences held by each listed company?

The GRA says it will periodically update the list as additional licences are issued.

That provision is important because it suggests the November 2 communication was not necessarily a final register of every operator that might eventually qualify for the financial year.

MOZZARTBET’S KSH256 MILLION MONEY-LAUNDERING BATTLE WITH THE GOVERNMENT

For MozzartBet, the licensing omission comes against the background of a particularly damaging legal battle involving the Assets Recovery Agency (ARA), the Directorate of Criminal Investigations and the courts.

On May 23, 2025, the Court of Appeal delivered a judgment that upheld the forfeiture of approximately KSh256 million connected to transactions involving MozzartBet Kenya Limited, Kimaco Connections Limited and Pescom Kenya.

The decision in Mozzartbet Kenya Limited v Assets Recovery Agency & 2 Others, Civil Appeals E250 and E313 of 2022, is among the most significant judicial findings involving a major betting operator in Kenya.

The dispute originated from payments MozzartBet said it had made to Kimaco Connections Limited under two agreements for the development, supply and commissioning of customised betting software.

MozzartBet sought to recover KSh256,051,910, arguing that the money represented legitimate commercial payments for software development services.

However, the Asset Recovery Agency questioned the legitimacy of the transactions and sought forfeiture of funds held in accounts associated with Kimaco and Pescom.

The High Court subsequently found that Kimaco was a shell company without the financial capacity to execute the alleged software contract.

The Court of Appeal upheld the forfeiture decision, finding that the evidence met the civil standard of proof, namely the balance of probabilities, under the Proceeds of Crime and Anti-Money Laundering Act.

The judges identified several troubling aspects of the transactions.

Among them were substantial transfers from Kimaco and associated accounts to individuals connected with MozzartBet.

The judgment specifically discussed payments involving individuals identified as Emmanuel Charumbira, Musa Cherutich Sirma and Branimir Melentijevic.

The court also examined payments allegedly connected to software invoices and other commercial arrangements for which satisfactory supporting evidence was not produced.

In one particularly significant finding, the judges observed that Kimaco had filed nil returns with the Kenya Revenue Authority during the period in question, despite the substantial commercial transactions attributed to it.

The court concluded that the evidence supported the forfeiture of the funds as proceeds of crime.

This was not merely an allegation made by a competitor or an anonymous social media account. It was a judicial determination following proceedings before the High Court and a subsequent appeal.

However, the distinction is important: the proceedings concerned civil recovery of suspected proceeds of crime, not a criminal conviction of MozzartBet or its directors.

The question now facing the GRA is whether the findings in that judgment have been adequately considered in MozzartBet’s current licensing assessment.

A company operating in a highly regulated financial activity cannot expect its regulatory history to be treated as irrelevant simply because it has an established customer base.

THE OLD LICENCE PROBLEM: MOZZARTBET STILL REFERENCING THE FORMER REGULATOR

Another issue emerging from the licensing dispute concerns the transition from the Betting Control and Licensing Board (BCLB) to the Gambling Regulatory Authority.

The Gambling Control Act, 2025, which came into operation on August 20, 2025, established the new regulatory framework and transformed the previous BCLB into the GRA.

This was intended to modernise gambling oversight, licensing, supervision and enforcement.

However, the transition has exposed a difficult question for operators that previously relied on licences issued under the old regulatory framework.

A review of MozzartBet’s Kenyan website conducted in October 2026 by PaybillKE found that the company was still displaying BCLB licence numbers BK 0001188 and PG 0001189.

The same review found that the operator was still identifying the former regulator rather than displaying the new GRA licensing details.

This does not automatically establish that the old licences became invalid on the date the new regulator was established. The critical question is whether MozzartBet subsequently obtained the necessary authorisation under the new framework.

But its absence from the November 2 list makes that question particularly urgent.

Other operators have similarly continued displaying legacy BCLB licensing information, suggesting that the transition has not been straightforward across the industry.

The regulator must explain whether these companies have pending applications, transitional approvals, outstanding compliance requirements or have simply failed to secure new licences.

A PREVIOUS LICENSING SHOWDOWN WITH THE KENYAN GOVERNMENT

MozzartBet’s latest licensing uncertainty is not its first confrontation with Kenyan authorities.

In July 2019, the government suspended the operating licences of 27 betting companies over outstanding renewal requirements and ongoing due diligence.

The affected operators included MozzartBet.

The BCLB also instructed mobile money providers, including Safaricom, Telkom and Airtel, to suspend payment channels belonging to the affected companies.

MozzartBet publicly disputed the purported suspension, saying it was unaware of any outstanding compliance complaint and that it was engaging the regulator.

The company pointed to its tax compliance documentation and maintained that it had no pending regulatory issue.

It subsequently announced that its licence had been renewed for the 2019/2020 financial year.

The episode demonstrated how dependent betting companies are on the goodwill and regulatory authorisation of government agencies and mobile money providers.

It also established a pattern that remains relevant today: licensing is not simply about having a functioning website, a recognisable brand or thousands of customers.

It is about satisfying the regulator’s requirements and maintaining the necessary approvals to conduct business.

BetPawa, another major name in Kenya’s betting industry, is also absent from the November 2 list.

A review of their publicly displayed licensing information by PaybillKE found that some operators continued referencing legacy BCLB licence numbers even as the new GRA framework took effect.

For example, MozzartBet displayed BK 0001188 and PG 0001189, while 22Bet displayed BK-0001293 and PG-0001294.

BetPawa displayed BK0001070 and PG0001071.

These historical numbers should not be confused with confirmation of a current 2026/2027 operating licence.

The absence of these companies from the GRA’s November 2 communication therefore raises questions about their applications and regulatory compliance.

It also presents a challenge to Safaricom.

If the regulator has instructed the telecommunications company to enforce licensing requirements, how will it distinguish between companies with fully approved licences, those awaiting processing and those whose applications have been rejected?

The GRA must provide clarity rather than leave customers and payment providers to interpret an evolving regulatory position.

THE REGULATORY TRANSITION IS ITSELF UNDER LEGAL PRESSURE

There is another important dimension to this story.

Kenya’s new gambling regulatory framework has faced litigation over the legality and implementation of its licensing regulations, particularly the increased fees and capital requirements imposed on operators.

In August 2026, the High Court allowed the GRA to implement licensing regulations and collect licensing fees pending determination of the underlying dispute, following an earlier suspension of parts of the framework.

The regulator had told the court that 246 licence applications were pending and could not be processed while the fee provisions were suspended.

The petitioners, meanwhile, challenged the increased costs, arguing that they could force smaller operators out of the market and undermine investment.

The court also acknowledged a significant transitional problem: the new Act did not provide a comprehensive licensing transition framework after the repeal of the previous legislation.

Parliament’s Committee on Delegated Legislation tracker, dated October 30, 2026, still records the 2026 gambling regulations as stayed by a court order.

The August court proceedings and the parliamentary tracker therefore need to be read together, rather than assuming that every aspect of the regulations has been conclusively settled.

This regulatory uncertainty matters because operators cannot be judged fairly without a clear explanation of the applicable licensing requirements, transitional arrangements and status of their applications.

THE BIGGER QUESTION: WHO IS PROTECTING KENYAN PUNTERS?

The licensing controversy goes beyond the commercial interests of betting companies.

Millions of Kenyans interact with betting platforms through mobile money services. Their deposits, withdrawals, personal information and transaction records are tied to companies whose regulatory status should be beyond doubt.

A company that accepts deposits from Kenyan customers must be accountable to Kenyan law.

The GRA’s decision to involve Safaricom in enforcing the licensing requirements is therefore significant.
