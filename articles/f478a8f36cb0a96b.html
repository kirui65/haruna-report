Mbadi, Oduor face sentencing over Kenya debt secrecy

WhatsAppFacebookX

High Court finds Treasury CS and Attorney General in contempt for defying orders to disclose sovereign bond agreements and spending.

National Treasury Cabinet Secretary John Mbadi and Attorney General Dorcas Oduor were yesterday found guilty of contempt of court by the High Court after failing to comply with orders requiring the government to disclose Kenya&#8217;s sovereign bond agreements and account for how the borrowed funds were spent, with both officials now facing personal sentencing on 27 November 2026.

High Court Judge Patricia Nyaunndi delivered the ruling following a contempt application by the Kenya Human Rights Commission and activist Wanjiru Gikonyo, National Coordinator of the Institute for Social Accountability — the same petitioners who first took the government to court in April 2022 demanding transparency over Kenya&#8217;s mounting public debt. The ruling is as significant for what it says about the state of Kenya&#8217;s institutions as it is about the specific officials named: two of the country&#8217;s most senior law officers have been found to have defied a binding court order, and they will now answer for it personally.

The legal origins of Thursday&#8217;s ruling stretch back over four years. In early 2022, the Kenya Human Rights Commission and Gikonyo formally requested records of all sovereign bonds floated by the Kenyan state over the preceding nine years, anchoring their demand in Article 201 of the Constitution, which mandates openness and accountability in all matters of public finance. When the National Treasury ignored the request, the petitioners moved to the High Court. In December 2024, Justice Lawrence Mugambi ruled in their favour, declaring the government&#8217;s secrecy unconstitutional and giving the state 45 days to produce two categories of information: all sovereign bond agreements and contracts entered into by the Republic of Kenya, and a full breakdown of how the proceeds from those bonds were spent.

Nearly two years later, neither set of disclosures had been made. Justice Nyaunndi&#8217;s contempt ruling is the direct consequence of that silence. The court has ordered both Mbadi and Oduor to appear in person before the High Court at noon on 27 November for sentencing — a requirement that underscores the personal nature of the accountability the bench is now imposing. In a further measure of the court&#8217;s displeasure, it ruled that the costs of the contempt application will be borne by the two officials in their personal capacities, not by the taxpayer. The state will not be permitted to absorb the financial consequences of its own defiance.

The significance of that last point should not be understated. Ordering public officials to pay legal costs personally rather than through government funds is a deliberate and pointed signal from the judiciary — a statement that contempt of court is not an institutional inconvenience to be managed through public resources but a personal breach of legal obligation with personal consequences. For a government that has repeatedly tested the boundaries of judicial authority, the November 27 sentencing will be an important moment of institutional reckoning.

Kenya&#8217;s public debt has been one of the most contested political and economic issues of the past decade. The country&#8217;s total debt stock has grown substantially across successive administrations, financed in part through a series of sovereign bond issuances that have attracted scrutiny from civil society, economists and opposition politicians alike. Questions about how the proceeds of those bonds were deployed — which projects received funding, which contracts were awarded, on what terms and to whom — have remained largely unanswered in the public domain. The court orders at the centre of Thursday&#8217;s contempt ruling were specifically designed to force those answers into the open. That they were not complied with for nearly two years speaks to the depth of the government&#8217;s reluctance to subject its borrowing record to public scrutiny.

For the Kenya Human Rights Commission and Gikonyo, Thursday&#8217;s ruling represents a significant, if incomplete, vindication. The judiciary has confirmed that the government&#8217;s continued secrecy is not merely a matter of administrative delay but a wilful defiance of constitutional and court-mandated obligations. &#8220;The Constitution is clear,&#8221; Gikonyo has previously stated in relation to the case. &#8220;Openness and accountability in public finance are not aspirational — they are mandatory.&#8221; The contempt finding gives that position the full force of judicial authority.

What happens on 27 November will determine the next chapter of this confrontation. The sentencing options available to Justice Nyaunndi range from fines to, in principle, committal — though the latter would represent an extraordinary escalation in the relationship between the judiciary and the executive. More likely is a structured order requiring compliance within a fixed and final deadline, backed by the credible threat of further and more severe consequences for continued defiance.

For ordinary Kenyans, whose taxes service a debt whose full terms they have never been permitted to see, the case is not abstract. It concerns the basic right to know how public money has been borrowed, on what conditions and to what ends. The High Court has now said, twice and unambiguously, that the government must answer those questions. Whether it finally does so will be the measure of whether Kenya&#8217;s constitutional architecture means what it says.
