Top 10 African Countries by Crypto Adoption: Nigeria Leads as Tanzania, Kenya and Rwanda Enter the Top 10

WhatsAppFacebookX

Nigeria has emerged as the leading African market in the crypto-adoption table published in the OECD’s Africa Capital Markets Report 2026, while Kenya, Tanzania and Rwanda have secured places among the ten countries displayed at the top of the continental ranking. Nigeria’s commanding position shows that demand is already established. The broader economic question is whether African governments can convert that demand into safer and more efficient financial infrastructure, without allowing speculative activity, cybercrime or unregulated foreign-currency substitutes to undermine households and the formal financial system.

The OECD table places Nigeria first in Africa with an adoption score of 0.45 and sixth globally. Morocco ranks second on the continent and 24th globally, followed by Kenya in third place and 25th worldwide.

Egypt occupies fourth position in Africa, South Africa fifth, Ghana sixth and Tunisia seventh. Tanzania, Rwanda and Zambia complete the ten countries displayed at the top of the OECD’s African table.

The ranking gives East Africa three places in the top ten. Kenya is the region’s strongest-performing market, while Tanzania and Rwanda rank eighth and ninth respectively. Their presence reflects the growing intersection between mobile money, digital payments, cross-border commerce and emerging virtual-asset services.

The ranking does not mean that crypto-assets are officially recognised as money in these countries. Nor does it show the percentage of citizens who own Bitcoin or other digital assets. It measures the intensity of crypto activity relative to the size and purchasing power of each economy.

Nigeria’s adoption score of 0.45 is more than three times the rounded score recorded by Morocco and Kenya, its closest African competitors. The country ranks sixth globally, behind India, the United States, Pakistan, Vietnam and Brazil in the underlying Chainalysis index.

Nigeria’s position reflects the scale of its digital economy, its large and youthful population, the strength of its fintech ecosystem and the economic pressures encouraging individuals and businesses to seek alternative ways of transferring and preserving value.

Foreign-exchange shortages, currency depreciation and inflation have increased demand for dollar-linked stablecoins and other digital assets. Some users turn to crypto platforms to receive international payments, pay overseas suppliers or protect part of their savings from movements in the naira.

The OECD also reports that Nigeria ranks third globally for transaction activity involving decentralised-finance protocols. These platforms allow users to trade, borrow, lend or transfer digital assets without relying entirely on a conventional financial intermediary.

Nigeria’s position should not be interpreted as evidence that cryptocurrency has replaced the naira. Much of the activity remains connected to investment, speculation and trading. Nevertheless, the ranking shows that digital assets have developed into a material part of the country’s wider financial landscape.

Nigeria has responded by moving from broad restrictions towards formal regulation. The Investments and Securities Act of 2025 brought crypto exchanges, issuers, custodians and other virtual-asset service providers within the country’s securities-regulation framework.

Morocco ranks second in the OECD’s displayed African table, with an adoption score of 0.14 and a global ranking of 24.

Its position is notable because Moroccan financial authorities have continued to treat virtual-currency transactions as unauthorised. Authorities have warned that using digital assets may breach foreign-exchange controls and financial regulations, while the country has not yet established a comprehensive licensing framework for crypto-asset service providers.

The apparent contradiction between restrictive regulation and high adoption illustrates an important feature of Africa’s digital-asset market. Consumer activity can continue through peer-to-peer networks, offshore exchanges and informal channels even where domestic institutions are not permitted to offer crypto services.

Restrictive rules may therefore reduce regulated activity without eliminating demand. They can also make it harder for authorities to collect market information, protect consumers and distinguish legitimate transactions from fraud or illicit financial activity.

Kenya is third in Africa and 25th globally, with an adoption score rounded to 0.14. Although Kenya and Morocco have the same displayed score, their unrounded results place Morocco one position ahead in the global index.

Kenya’s ranking reflects the country’s established mobile-money culture and wider digital-finance ecosystem. Consumers and businesses are already accustomed to storing, transferring and receiving value through mobile platforms, reducing the behavioural barrier to experimenting with stablecoins and other digital assets.

The OECD identifies Kenya as one of the African markets where users can receive and convert stablecoins into local currency through services connected to mobile-payment systems. It also cites emerging Bitcoin-based financing services for micro, small and medium-sized enterprises in Kenya, Nigeria, Tanzania and Uganda.

Kenya adopted the Virtual Asset Service Providers Act in 2025 and implementing regulations in 2026. The framework introduced licensing requirements for exchanges, brokers, custodial-wallet providers, advisers and crypto-payment processors.

The regulations recognise stablecoins within the broader virtual-asset market, although that does not automatically make them legal tender or give them the same status as the Kenyan shilling.

For Kenyan businesses, the more economically significant opportunity may be in regulated cross-border payments, remittances and trade settlement rather than speculative token trading. Kenya’s strong commercial connections across East Africa could make it an important regional base for compliant virtual-asset and blockchain services.

Egypt ranks fourth in Africa with an adoption score of 0.13 and a global position of 26. Its strong ranking also exists alongside a restrictive regulatory environment.

Egyptian law prohibits the issuance, trading and promotion of crypto-assets without approval from the Central Bank of Egypt. The OECD says no such approvals had been granted by September 2026, leaving the country with what the report describes as a de facto prohibition.

Continued activity despite those restrictions suggests that demand is being driven partly through informal or offshore channels. Currency pressure, inflation and restrictions on access to foreign exchange can increase interest in digital alternatives, even when domestic financial institutions cannot legally facilitate them.

South Africa ranks fifth in Africa and 28th globally, with a score of 0.12. Unlike several other highly ranked African markets, South Africa has established a formal licensing and supervisory approach through its existing financial-services framework.

Its relatively mature banking system, capital markets and fintech industry provide a stronger institutional base for integrating digital assets with regulated financial services. South Africa and Nigeria are also emerging as major African markets for stablecoins.

The OECD cites survey evidence suggesting that close to 80 per cent of surveyed crypto owners in the two countries held stablecoins, while more than 75 per cent of stablecoin holders intended to increase their holdings.

Ghana is sixth in Africa and 40th globally, with an adoption score of 0.08.

Like Nigeria, Ghana has experienced currency and inflation pressures that have increased interest in digital assets as alternative stores of value and channels for receiving international payments. The country’s established mobile-money market has also made consumers more comfortable with digital financial products.

Ghana enacted the Virtual Asset Service Providers Act in 2025, creating an activity-based licensing and supervision system involving the Bank of Ghana and the Securities and Exchange Commission.

The framework covers exchanges, custody services, digital wallets, asset transfers and issuance. Stablecoins fall within the general virtual-asset regulatory perimeter, although Ghana has not established a completely separate stablecoin regime.

Tunisia ranks seventh in Africa and 52nd globally, with a score of 0.05. The country has not introduced dedicated legislation or a comprehensive licensing framework for virtual-asset service providers.

As in Morocco and Egypt, Tunisia’s ranking shows that the level of market activity cannot be used as a measure of regulatory openness. High adoption can develop partly because users are responding to limitations within the conventional financial and foreign-exchange systems.

Tanzania is eighth in the OECD’s displayed African ranking and 73rd globally, with an adoption score of 0.03.

Its inclusion in the top ten is economically significant because the Bank of Tanzania has repeatedly warned that crypto-assets are not legal tender. Regulated financial institutions have also been cautioned against facilitating transactions that have not received the required authorisation.

Tanzania has nevertheless established a fintech sandbox through which eligible financial innovations can be tested in a controlled environment. The sandbox can accommodate some distributed-ledger applications, but the OECD says publicly available official information does not confirm that stablecoins have received general approval.

Tanzania’s ranking suggests that digital-asset demand is developing outside the conventional banking system, particularly through peer-to-peer transactions and internationally accessible platforms.

For policymakers, the challenge is to decide how legitimate blockchain and digital-asset innovation can be tested without exposing consumers, the banking system or the shilling to unmanaged risks.

A prolonged absence of clear rules may not stop participation. Instead, it could push activity towards offshore exchanges and informal networks that are more difficult to monitor, tax or subject to consumer-protection requirements.

The country’s established mobile-money ecosystem and growing cross-border trade with Kenya, Rwanda, Burundi, Uganda, Zambia and the Democratic Republic of Congo could eventually support regulated uses involving remittances, trade settlement and business payments.

Rwanda is ninth in the OECD table and 93rd globally, with an adoption score of 0.02.

The country introduced its first formal framework for crypto-asset businesses in 2026. The Capital Market Authority is responsible for licensing and supervising service providers in cooperation with the National Bank of Rwanda.

The framework covers service provision, issuance, consumer protection and financial-integrity risks. However, using crypto-assets for payments requires explicit central-bank approval.

Rwanda’s approach draws a line between allowing regulated virtual-asset investment services and permitting privately issued assets to perform the functions of national money.

For Rwanda, the potential opportunity lies in using its strong digital-government and financial-technology infrastructure to attract compliant service providers. The country will still need to address cybersecurity, custody, taxation and the treatment of cross-border transactions.

Zambia occupies tenth position in the OECD’s African table, with a score of 0.01 and a global ranking of 94.

As of April 2026, Zambia was developing a register for crypto-asset service providers. However, the OECD says crypto-assets remained unregulated and their use was not permitted under the existing framework.

Zambia’s inclusion demonstrates that measurable market activity can emerge before a country develops a complete regulatory system. It also shows why authorities need reliable data on domestic participation before designing licensing, taxation and consumer-protection requirements.

The OECD ranking is based on the Chainalysis 2025 Global Crypto Adoption Index, which evaluates 151 countries through four usage-based measures.

The index assesses the value received through centralised crypto services, retail transactions below US$10,000, decentralised-finance activity and institutional-sized transactions above US$1 million. The results are adjusted using population, purchasing power and GDP per capita.

This means the index is designed to identify countries where crypto activity is significant relative to the size and income of the economy. It is not a ranking of the largest absolute crypto markets or the countries holding the greatest quantity of digital assets.

Chainalysis estimates geographical activity using blockchain information and web-traffic patterns. It acknowledges that virtual private networks can obscure users’ locations, although it argues that the size of its dataset reduces the overall effect of such distortions.

Although the ranking covers the broader crypto market, stablecoins may provide the most immediate economic use case for African households and businesses.

Traditional remittances to sub-Saharan Africa cost an average of 6.4 per cent in 2025, according to data cited by the OECD. Regulated stablecoin transfers could potentially lower costs and shorten settlement times for diaspora households, exporters, freelancers and small businesses.

That potential has not yet translated into widespread payment use. The OECD estimates that approximately 89 per cent of identified global stablecoin activity is connected to crypto trading, while payments represent only about 2 per cent.

The figures show that digital-asset growth should not automatically be presented as evidence of a transformation in trade finance or financial inclusion. Much of the current activity still involves trading, investment and arbitrage.

Stablecoins also depend on issuers maintaining sufficient high-quality reserves and allowing holders to redeem their assets at the promised value. A loss of confidence or shortage of liquid backing assets can cause a supposedly stable token to fall below its intended price.

The top ten countries represent widely different policy positions. Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana and Rwanda have established formal regulatory frameworks. Tanzania permits limited financial-technology experimentation while maintaining that crypto-assets are not legal tender. Morocco, Egypt and Zambia retain restrictive approaches, while Tunisia has not established a dedicated licensing regime.

This fragmentation increases costs for companies attempting to operate across several African markets. A licence issued in Kenya, Nigeria or Rwanda does not automatically authorise a platform to serve consumers in Tanzania, Ghana or South Africa.

Regional regulatory alignment could encourage legitimate investment, strengthen consumer protection and support cross-border interoperability. It could also make it easier to identify and remove fraudulent operators.

The risks remain substantial. The OECD estimates that crypto-related cyberattacks caused approximately US$16.5 billion in global losses over the past decade. About US$9 billion was associated with attacks on wallet holders, including stolen private keys and compromised digital wallets.

Nigeria’s commanding lead shows that African demand for digital assets is already established. Kenya, Tanzania and Rwanda’s inclusion in the displayed top ten demonstrates that East Africa is becoming an important part of that market.

The next test is whether governments can convert adoption into safer payment, investment and financing infrastructure, or whether activity will remain dominated by speculation, offshore platforms and regulatory uncertainty.
