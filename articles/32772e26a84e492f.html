Kenya Needs Its Own AI Models, Data Centres And Energy, Ndemo Says

WhatsAppFacebookX

Kenya must build its own artificial intelligence infrastructure and train models in local languages if it is to retain control of its data, develop relevant AI applications and capture more economic value from the technology, Dr Bitange Ndemo has said.

Speaking during a session on Sovereign AI at the CIO100 Symposium & Awards 2026 in Diani, the former Ambassador to Belgium and the European Union argued that relying on foreign AI models exposes African countries to external decisions over access, data and the technologies their economies increasingly depend on.

“Everybody can actually make their own AI which understands the local context,” Ndemo said, arguing that models must be trained to understand local laws, cultures and languages.

He described AI as a child’s brain that needs to be trained and shaped by its environment. A model developed elsewhere, he argued, may not adequately understand the differences between Kenyan, Tanzanian and Ugandan law, or the social and cultural contexts in which people use technology.

For Ndemo, sovereignty goes beyond hosting data locally. It requires countries to develop the supporting infrastructure, including data centres, reliable energy, connectivity, skilled engineers and locally trained language models.

Local languages are central to AI adoption

Ndemo said the limitations of foreign models become particularly significant when AI is expected to address problems rooted in local economic and social realities.

He argued that African languages do not always have direct equivalents for concepts such as data, productivity and predictability. Without the vocabulary to communicate these ideas accurately, he said, AI systems risk reproducing gaps in how development challenges are understood and addressed.

He used poverty as an example, arguing that efforts to address it have often failed to communicate the practical knowledge people need to improve their livelihoods.

In agriculture, he said, farmers need information that helps them use fewer resources to produce more. Locally trained models could help communicate such knowledge in languages and contexts that communities understand.

Ndemo also pointed to education, arguing that learning in a familiar language can improve comprehension. Personalised AI, he said, could adapt how information is delivered to an individual&#8217;s pace of understanding, potentially reaching learners who struggle in conventional classroom settings.

He said locally developed models could also help bridge the gap between formal education and practical knowledge, using the example of rural builders who understand roof angles through experience even when they may not connect that knowledge to classroom mathematics.

Sovereignty requires infrastructure and energy

Ndemo warned that dependence on foreign AI providers creates strategic risks for countries that lack control over the models they use.

He raised the possibility of an external provider restricting access to its services following a political or commercial decision, leaving businesses and public institutions without systems on which they have come to rely.

He also questioned the value African countries receive when they provide data that can be used elsewhere to develop commercial products, including medicines.

His argument was that African countries should retain greater control over the data and infrastructure needed to develop their own AI capabilities.

Energy, he said, is a critical part of that ambition. He urged African governments and investors to direct more capital towards electricity generation, arguing that data centres and GPU infrastructure require reliable and affordable power.

Citing Ethiopia&#8217;s investments in electricity generation, Ndemo said the country had moved ahead with large-scale energy projects that could support industrial activity and emerging technology industries.

He argued that Kenya should use available land and energy resources to attract infrastructure investment, create employment and support a growing cloud economy.

Building a talent pipeline and local AI industry

Ndemo said sovereign AI would also require sustained investment in engineers and other technical professionals who can build, train and maintain local systems.

He argued that developing local language models could create opportunities for African startups, content creators and technology businesses, while allowing countries to address challenges that imported systems may not adequately understand.

Among the potential applications he cited was healthcare. He described the use of smartphone-based tools to help health workers identify possible tuberculosis cases, illustrating how AI could support earlier diagnosis and intervention.

He also said locally developed systems could help create jobs by enabling businesses to build products and services around models trained for African markets.

Ndemo disclosed that he was working towards developing a Kenyan AI model and was seeking local partners willing to test and support the initiative before wider commercial participation.

He said the intention was to make a basic model available for people to begin using, with a broader national AI service offering that could support developers and businesses. He invited interested organisations to engage with the initiative, including through potential subscriptions to solutions built on the model.

His message to technology leaders was that AI sovereignty should not be reduced to a debate about foreign versus local software. It should be treated as a national capability involving energy, infrastructure, data, skills and language.

“Everything needed already exists. Only the model is missing,” Ndemo said.
