Marc Marquez retires after eighth MotoGP title in Valencia

WhatsAppFacebookX

The greatest motorcycle racer of his generation closes a career defined by genius, injury, comeback and history — leaving MotoGP without its most compelling protagonist.

Marc Marquez announced his retirement from MotoGP on Friday at the Circuit Ricardo Tormo in Valencia, ending one of the most extraordinary careers in motorsport history after the Spaniard secured a record-equalling eighth premier-class world championship in Portugal with a round to spare.

The announcement stunned a paddock that had expected Marquez to lead the sport into its new 850cc era. He had signed a contract extension with Ducati earlier in 2026, due to run until 2028. What followed was a shoulder surgery — his fourth significant operation — a fractured foot, an absence from his home Grand Prix and a 102-point deficit in the championship standings. That he responded by winning six of the next nine rounds and retaining the title makes the retirement&#8217;s timing all the more remarkable. He leaves not in decline but at the precise moment of his greatest triumph.

&#8220;Thank you to everyone who&#8217;s been part of the journey,&#8221; he said in a video recorded in the Valencia garage. &#8220;I hope you&#8217;ve enjoyed it as much as I have. This will be my last race in MotoGP. We made history.&#8221;

They did. Eight world championships. A debut title in 2013 at 20 years old, making him the youngest premier-class champion the sport had seen. Six further crowns with Honda before injury, serial surgery and mechanical decline stripped him of everything except the stubbornness to continue. A move to Gresini Racing on year-old Ducati machinery. A resurrection. Then the factory Ducati seat, a seventh title in Japan in 2025 — 2,184 days after his sixth — and now an eighth that required him to overturn a deficit that would have ended most riders&#8217; seasons entirely.

Marquez said he made the decision to retire during the Japanese Grand Prix, before the season&#8217;s conclusion, as the cumulative physical and mental cost of repeated injuries began to outweigh his appetite for competition. &#8220;Sometimes you need to understand the situation, but it&#8217;s true that the situation this year was on the limit,&#8221; he said. Brief recurrences of double vision at the Indonesian and French Grands Prix — the neurological legacy of a catastrophic 2011 crash — made him more conservative in the season&#8217;s second half. &#8220;It was one of the main reasons why I controlled the risk and why I crashed less,&#8221; he acknowledged.

The announcement brought tears to the Ducati garage and to the grandstands at Valencia. Jorge Martin, his principal title rival, looked stunned. Alex Marquez, told of the decision weeks earlier in Japan, watched with the quiet gravity of a man who had spent a career observing his brother from an impossible distance.

The arc of Marquez&#8217;s story is unlike any other in the sport&#8217;s modern era. He arrived in 2013 and immediately won, as though the premier class were simply the next natural step. By 2019, six championships in and apparently unbeatable, prolonged dominance seemed inevitable. Then Jerez in 2020: a crash, a broken arm, a botched recovery, three more surgeries and years spent riding compromised equipment into circuits he had once dismantled with total authority.

He considered stopping. He admitted as much. That he chose instead to change manufacturer, rebuild on slower machinery and reconstruct his belief from the ground up is the detail that separates his story from the standard narrative of sporting greatness. When he finally won again — ending a 1,043-day drought in Aragon — it carried the weight of everything that had come before it.

The symbolic moment came in Portugal, where Giacomo Agostini presented him with a commemorative golden helmet as a graphic of a goat — shorthand for the greatest of all time — appeared on the screen behind him. Marquez, characteristically, resisted the superlative. &#8220;I do not agree that I&#8217;m the greatest rider. I don&#8217;t like it. I&#8217;m one of the greatest, yes, because the numbers are there,&#8221; he said.

The numbers, and the story behind them, suggest the distinction is academic.

Valencia&#8217;s final lap will represent the last time the most gifted, most maddening and most compelling motorcycle racer of his generation lines up on a MotoGP grid. After everything — the titles, the surgeries, the comebacks — Marc Marquez is going out entirely on his own terms.
