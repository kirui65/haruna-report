Gachagua accuses IEBC of state capture ahead of 2027 polls

WhatsAppFacebookX

The impeached former Deputy President fires his sharpest warning yet — at the IEBC, President Ruto, and anyone who thinks they can steal the vote

Former Deputy President Rigathi Gachagua returned to prime-time national television on Wednesday night with his most combative performance yet, declaring on Citizen TV&#8217;s JKLive that no one would steal the 10 August 2027 General Election on his watch — and accusing the country&#8217;s electoral commission of operating under state capture at the direction of President William Ruto.

The interview, broadcast to millions of Kenyans still weighing the country&#8217;s fractured political landscape, was not merely a television appearance. It was, in effect, the formal opening salvo of the most consequential election campaign Kenya has seen since 2002. Speaking in his capacity as leader of the Democracy for the Citizens Party, Gachagua was blunt: no one would be able to rig the election if he was involved, and he would be watching every step of the process.

&#8220;I&#8217;m an expert in elections; I&#8217;ve never lost a presidential election. I have supported presidential candidates even when it looked like it was not fashionable to support Ruto. The day I agreed to support him, he won,&#8221; he said, drawing an unmistakable line under his claim to be the opposition&#8217;s most formidable electoral strategist.

Kenya&#8217;s road to August 2027 is already deeply contested. At its centre stands Gachagua — impeached, unbowed, and operating with the energy of a man who believes history dealt him an injustice he intends to correct at the ballot box. His audience understands the subtext. The Mt Kenya region, which he commands as Kenya&#8217;s most populous and historically decisive voting bloc, is watching every move.

The Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission did not escape the night unscathed. Gachagua accused the IEBC chairman Erastus Ethekon of operating under government influence, alleging the electoral agency was being controlled by the Ministry of Interior. &#8220;The chairman is under state capture; IEBC is run by the Ministry of Interior,&#8221; he said during the JKLive interview. The IEBC has consistently denied any political interference and has stated publicly that it has nothing to hide.

The interview followed a dramatic day in which Gachagua, alongside United Green Movement leader David Maraga and Democratic Party leader Justin Muturi, boycotted a consultative meeting with the IEBC, claiming it had been organised not by the commission but by the State Department for Interior. &#8220;We are not willing to engage in a charade. The meeting this afternoon has been organised by the State Department of Interior. Why is William Ruto speaking for IEBC?&#8221; he said at a press briefing earlier in the day.

Gachagua announced that the United Opposition would mobilise Kenyans in January 2027 to engage the commission directly should concerns remain unaddressed. &#8220;William Ruto cannot threaten us. Next year in January, we will organise Kenyans to talk to IEBC directly,&#8221; he warned.

The accusations are serious — and structurally significant. Kenya&#8217;s elections have been disputed before, most catastrophically in 2007 and most controversially in 2017. The spectre of a 2027 contest in which the main opposition does not trust the umpire before a single ballot has been cast raises questions that go far beyond political theatre.

Gachagua has been building this case for weeks. Earlier this month, speaking at the opening of DCP&#8217;s Nairobi County offices in Kasarani, he accused the Registrar of Persons of deliberately delaying the issuance of national identity cards to young people in perceived opposition strongholds, claiming applicants in government-aligned areas received their IDs within two to three days while those in opposition areas waited up to seven months. The government dismissed the claims.

On Wednesday night he went further still, alleging that Interior Principal Secretary Raymond Omollo had set aside KSh15 billion to finance political candidates targeting leaders allied to the late Raila Odinga in the 2027 election, with several senior government and parastatal officials expected to resign before 10 February 2027 to seek elective office under the alleged arrangement. Neither Omollo nor the government has responded to the specific allegations.

What gives Gachagua&#8217;s warnings their unusual weight is not merely his rhetoric — it is his record and his reach. Since his impeachment, he has constructed a political machine of remarkable speed and discipline. He has sought counsel from Kikuyu elders under the Kiama Kia Kiama leadership, whose guidance, he says, is shaping his strategy towards what he calls the formation of the &#8220;sixth administration.&#8221; He has opened party offices across the country, fielded candidates successfully in by-elections, and built what observers describe as the most organised opposition structure Kenya has seen heading into a general election.

In October, speaking on KTN, he predicted that the 2027 election would mirror Kenya&#8217;s 2002 moment — when opposition parties united behind Mwai Kibaki to end KANU&#8217;s decades-long grip on power. He warned that politicians who reject a joint opposition candidate risk political isolation. He has not publicly declared whether he will be that candidate — or whether he will play kingmaker instead — but the ambiguity appears deliberate.

On JKLive, Gachagua addressed the question of his eligibility directly, citing Article 99(3) of the Constitution to argue that he would qualify to be cleared as a presidential candidate. His lawyers have been pursuing the matter through the courts, and observers expect a ruling on his eligibility to become one of the defining legal battles of the pre-election period.

He has also prepared for the possibility of disqualification, revealing that he has identified a pool of young leaders capable of carrying the Mt Kenya region&#8217;s presidential bid if courts rule against him, dismissing suggestions that such a ruling would leave the region politically leaderless.

The political arithmetic of 2027 remains fluid. President Ruto retains the advantages of incumbency, state resources, and a party — the United Democratic Alliance — with deep organisational roots. But the economic pain of the past two years, the Gen Z uprising of 2024 that forced a cabinet reshuffle, and the continuing cost-of-living pressures have dented his approval ratings considerably. For many Kenyans, 2027 is not yet decided.

What Wednesday&#8217;s interview confirmed is that the campaign is now fully under way — raw, loud, and deeply personal. Gachagua is no longer simply a former official making allegations from the margins. He is the opposition&#8217;s most prominent voice, its most effective fundraiser, and arguably its most feared strategist. For a country with Kenya&#8217;s electoral history, the combination of a motivated opposition, a disputed umpire, and a sitting president fighting for his political survival makes August 2027 a date every Kenyan is already counting down to.

Whether the vote is clean, credible, and peaceful when it arrives will depend on choices made in the months between now and then. If Wednesday night&#8217;s interview is any guide, those months will not be quiet.
