Ruto’s State of the Nation: Billions promised

WhatsAppFacebookX

President William Ruto used his fourth address to Parliament to announce KSh 61.8B in debt settlements, healthcare gains and a major northern road — while firing an unmistakable political shot at Rigathi Gachagua ahead of 2027

President William Ruto delivered his fourth State of the Nation Address to Parliament on Wednesday, announcing the settlement of KSh 61.8 billion in long-outstanding government obligations, pledging police accountability reforms and issuing a pointed, if unnamed, rebuke of former Deputy President Rigathi Gachagua&#8217;s campaign to consolidate the Mt Kenya region behind his Democracy for the Citizens Party ahead of the 10 August 2027 General Election.

The address, delivered to a joint sitting of the National Assembly and the Senate in Nairobi, covered ground ranging from university and hospital debts to highway expansion, healthcare registration and examination integrity — but it was the political undercurrent that gave the speech its sharpest edge. With Kenya&#8217;s electoral clock ticking toward August 2027, Ruto used the constitutional platform of Parliament to frame the coming contest on his terms: a debate about policy, delivery and evidence, not personality and regional loyalty.

&#8220;No community should be reduced to a voting bloc. No region should be asked to surrender its judgement to a personality,&#8221; he told lawmakers, in remarks whose target, while unnamed, was unmistakable. Gachagua has spent recent weeks describing Mt Kenya as DCP territory, warning residents against incumbent legislators allied to the ruling United Democratic Alliance and declaring a political &#8220;transfer window&#8221; open until 31 December. On Tuesday, one day before Ruto&#8217;s address, Gachagua received three sitting MPs at his Wamunyoro residence — Gatundu South&#8217;s Gabriel Kagombe, Nyeri Woman Representative Rahab Mukami and Laikipia West&#8217;s Wachira Karani — and declared DCP &#8220;the party of the moment and the future.&#8221;

Ruto&#8217;s response was measured but deliberate. He called on all political actors — beginning with his own administration — to submit to a standard of evidence-based accountability. &#8220;Those who offer alternatives must explain their proposals. All of us must respect the people&#8217;s right to question, scrutinise and choose,&#8221; he said, challenging rivals to articulate how they would lower electricity costs, finance infrastructure, expand employment and improve healthcare. It was a calculated pivot: shifting the 2027 conversation from the grievance politics that Gachagua has cultivated toward a terrain where an incumbent with a delivery record believes he holds the advantage.

On financial settlements, Ruto announced the approval of KSh 18 billion owed to public and private universities and KSh 16 billion owed to hospitals under the now-defunct National Hospital Insurance Fund. Both payments, he said, were undergoing processing at the National Treasury and would be disbursed within weeks. He also announced the settlement of 28,726 verified pending bills amounting to KSh 27.8 billion, covering claims dating back to 2005. &#8220;These payments are undergoing the due process at the National Treasury for payment in the coming weeks,&#8221; he said, arguing that the disbursements would restore cash flow to businesses, protect jobs and ease financial pressure across multiple sectors.

In education, the President cited the recruitment of 100,000 teachers over four years against a shortage of 116,000 when his administration assumed office, with a further 20,000 to be recruited. He said the government had constructed 23,000 classrooms, was equipping 1,600 laboratories and had increased the annual education budget from KSh 521 billion in 2022/23 to KSh 784 billion currently. Notably, he revealed that for the first time in 65 years, Kenya&#8217;s national examination papers had been printed locally — a quiet but symbolically significant assertion of institutional capacity. Some 3.6 million candidates were sitting the KPSEA, KJSEA and KCSE examinations, he added, in conditions described as calm and orderly.

Healthcare figures dominated a significant portion of the address. Ruto said 33.7 million Kenyans had registered with the Social Health Authority, compared to approximately eight million under NHIF in 2022. He cited 53,465 patients receiving cancer treatment, 24,468 undergoing dialysis and 89 who had received kidney transplants under the scheme. &#8220;Through SHA, cover treatment costs are being financed on behalf of citizens, reducing the amounts that families would otherwise have to raise from either their savings, borrow from relatives, or obtain through sale of their assets,&#8221; he said. He acknowledged the troubled transition from NHIF to SHA but maintained that the long-term objective remained the progressive removal of catastrophic medical expenses from household budgets.

On infrastructure, Ruto confirmed that plans to dual the Nairobi–Mombasa Highway — approximately 460 kilometres — had reached an advanced stage, with the Kenya National Highways Authority estimating costs at around KSh 300 billion. He said the project would proceed without new taxes or additional public debt, though the proposed toll-road model has drawn scrutiny from the Motorists Association of Kenya, which has called for full public disclosure of the financing structure. Ruto also announced the Northern Kenya Gateway Corridor, a 750-kilometre road linking Isiolo, Wajir and Mandera — a project he acknowledged had attracted widespread mockery when first proposed. &#8220;Some have lived their entire lives without a single tarmacked road reaching their communities. Their entire lives,&#8221; he said, invoking the equity imperative that has become a recurring theme of his administration.

The most consequential passage of the address, however, may prove to be his remarks on police accountability — a subject that has shadowed his presidency since the June 2024 anti-Finance Bill protests. The Independent Policing Oversight Authority has documented 127 deaths across three major protest periods, while Missing Voices recorded 24 police-related killings in May 2026 alone. Ruto acknowledged the pain of those events, confirmed a KSh 2 billion compensation allocation and said KSh 1.2 billion had already been paid to 1,476 verified beneficiaries. He was unequivocal that compensation did not replace criminal accountability. &#8220;Investigations must continue, and prosecutions must follow for violations of the law, wherever the evidence warrants,&#8221; he said.

His pledge to strengthen de-escalation procedures, oversight mechanisms and professional standards within the police service will be measured against a record that rights organisations have consistently found wanting. IPOA has cited forensic bottlenecks and evidentiary challenges; by June 2026, only three cases had reached the courts.

As State of the Nation addresses go, Wednesday&#8217;s was dense with numbers, ambitious in scope and strategically timed. With barely eight months to the 10 August 2027 election, every figure announced, every road promised and every political line drawn carries a dual purpose — governance and campaign. Ruto knows it. So does Gachagua.

The question Kenya will spend the next eight months answering is which of them is right.
