Smallholder farmers in Limuru and Karatina are deploying low-cost erosion-control methods ahead of the rains season.

They are undertaking these measures following devastating losses during the 2024 floods that killed more than 230 people and stripped farmland of fertile topsoil across the country.

Elizabeth Itotia, who farms five acres on a slope in Limuru, Kiambu County , estimates she lost KSh 200,000 worth of produce when the 2024 rains destroyed her kale, spinach, cabbage, potatoes, and indigenous vegetables.

The floods also stripped her fields of nutrient-rich topsoil, forcing her family to source replacement soil from other farms at additional cost and labour.

According to a report by The Associated Press, this season, Itotia has planted Napier grass and bamboo along erosion-prone sections of the farm, using the plants&#039; root systems to stabilise the soil.

She has also placed sand-filled sacks inside shallow trenches to slow surface runoff. Her neighbour Esther Njoki has adopted a comparable method.

In Karatina, Nyeri County , coffee farmer Moses Muhoro is digging trenches and packing sacks with soil to limit runoff after the 2024 floods removed the productive topsoil from his land, reducing his coffee output the following season.

He added that many farmers in the area suffer avoidable losses because they receive timely information too late.

Samuel Munyiri, an agronomist at Osho Chemical Industries, confirmed that the sandbag technique is effective at reducing runoff losses, particularly in areas where small erosion channels are already forming.

He recommended terracing, gabion construction, contour planting, and the use of cover crops as more permanent solutions and advised farmers to shift towards crops that can tolerate prolonged wet conditions, such as corn, wheat, and barley.

The timing of this season&#039;s rains is particularly sensitive because the short rains overlap with Kenya&#039;s corn harvest.

Corn planted during the long rains is typically collected as the short rains begin, and excessive moisture at that stage raises the risk of aflatoxin contamination if the grain cannot dry adequately.

The latter two regions are among the country&#039;s principal food-producing zones, meaning significant losses there would carry national food security implications.
