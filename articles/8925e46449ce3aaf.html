Kenya’s new cancer rules set treatment deadlines and track SHA cover

WhatsAppFacebookX

The rules add treatment deadlines and digital tracking, but the reports leave enforcement and the system’s rollout unclear.

Kenya’s 2026 cancer guidelines set deadlines for diagnosis and treatment and link cancer care to a digital system intended to track use of the Ksh800,000 SHA oncology benefit . The Ministry of Health says the rules will standardise care and improve accountability; patients have complained that delayed approvals and hospital charges can leave them without cover before treatment is complete.

The guidelines are the first major revision of Kenya’s cancer treatment protocols since 2019 . Under the new timelines, adults should receive a diagnosis within 60 days of first contact with a healthcare provider, and children within 30 days. After diagnosis, patients should be assessed at a designated or specialised cancer treatment centre within 14 days and start treatment within 31 days of registration.

The timelines are intended to cut waits across consultations, testing and referrals. The reports do not say how compliance will be measured, or what consequences facilities will face if patients miss those deadlines.

Cancer treatment will be connected to SHA’s digital oncology module. The system is intended to create a unified patient record that can be accessed across facilities, reducing duplicate records and helping care continue when patients move between hospitals. Health Cabinet Secretary Aden Duale said it would also improve coordination and management of the oncology benefit.

Patients have raised concerns that the Ksh800,000 allocation is exhausted before treatment ends, as well as about delayed approvals, inflated bills and procedures they consider unnecessary. One patient quoted by People Daily described waiting at hospital while approval remained pending. SHA Chairman Abdi Mohamed said the authority had received complaints about questionable charges, including consultations allegedly billed when no doctor was present.

The Ministry and SHA have warned contracted facilities against using up a patient’s allocation on unnecessary services or billing for care that was not provided. The government says facilities must account for their charges. The reports do not explain how patients can challenge a bill or secure treatment when a disagreement over charges or approval arises.

The revised guidelines include molecular profiling, targeted therapy and immunotherapy—approaches the reports say were not widely embedded in the 2019 protocols. Molecular profiling examines a tumour’s genetic characteristics to help guide treatment; targeted therapy acts on particular abnormalities in cancer cells, while immunotherapy helps the immune system recognise and fight cancer.

The two People Daily accounts give slightly different estimates of the annual cancer burden: one says more than 44,000 new cases , the other says nearly 45,000. Both describe cancer as a major and growing burden. Neither provides a breakdown by cancer type, county or access to the newer treatments.

There is also no implementation timetable in the reports, nor a named body responsible for checking whether hospitals meet the new deadlines. A unified record and clearer rules may help, but the coverage does not establish whether the digital module is already operating or how quickly facilities will be connected.

Only People Daily’s account is available here, so there is no reporting from another outlet to compare against. Its two reports overlap substantially; the longer account adds direct remarks from Duale, Mohamed and a patient. The next test is whether the deadlines and billing safeguards change what patients experience at facilities—not just what the guidelines prescribe.
