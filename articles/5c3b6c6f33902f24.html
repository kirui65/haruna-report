Kenya drops its FlexPay case, the third fintech reversal since 2022

WhatsAppFacebookX

FlexPay, the trading name of Flexitech Group Limited, ran a save-now, buy-later service with Naivas Supermarkets under a seven-year agreement signed in February 2021 and due to run until 2028. Shoppers paid in instalments at Naivas tills, FlexPay kept a 5% commission, and the rest was supposed to reach Naivas within 24 hours.

The arrangement worked for about five years before Naivas said FlexPay stopped remitting collections in early 2026, leaving a shortfall of Sh31.2 million, roughly $240,000. The Directorate of Public Prosecutions recommended theft by agent charges in August.

Detectives arreste d two of the four executives eventually named, Martin Kariuki Maina and Johnson Gituma Mwangi, on 1 September. The other two, chief growth officer Dennis Karanu Mwangi and director Richard Machomba, also faced possible prosecution.

The High Court declined in early October to halt the prosecution , ruling that reputational harm alone does not justify stopping a criminal case already filed.

The case turned anyway. In a letter dated 6 November, the Office of the Director of Public Prosecutions reversed its own decision , telling the Nairobi Regional Criminal Investigation Officer that a further review of the Directorate of Criminal Investigations&#8217; evidence found the dispute civil in nature.

It directed that the file be closed with no further police action and that Naivas be advised to seek civil redress instead. The partnership that had processed more than Sh3 billion in customer payments over five years was already over by the time that letter went out.

Four days later, High Court judge Roseline Aburili barred police and prosecutors from arresting, detaining or charging the four executives while a separate constitutional petition they had filed, accusing Naivas of using police to recover a commercial debt, is heard.

Public interest is not merely served by securing convictions at all costs, it is primarily anchored on the integrity of the administration of justice. The public must have confidence that the criminal process is even handed.

FlexPay disputed being an agent of Naivas at all, telling the court it was a corporate customer operating a credit account, buying goods on credit and settling the balance periodically.

It counterclaimed that Naivas owed it Sh24 million in unreimbursed customer loyalty points and more than Sh300 million in losses it blamed on price manipulation by Naivas staff, plus a further Sh3.07 million it says it paid Naivas during the dispute but was never credited.

Neither the prosecutors&#8217; decision nor the High Court ruling settled which side&#8217;s financial claims are correct. Both remain open questions for whatever court eventually hears the underlying debt.

Kenya&#8217;s Asset Recovery Agency filed a money laundering and card fraud suit against the Nigerian fintech Kora and a second firm, Kandon Technologies, in July 2022. The court froze both companies&#8217; accounts for six months while the agency investigated.

By November, the Directorate of Criminal Investigations told the court the allegations were not established and asked that its communication be treated as final. Kora&#8217;s chief operating officer said the company had always maintained its innocence.

The Asset Recovery Agency also filed a money laundering case against Flutterwave in 2022, alleging that funds in its Kenyan accounts were proceeds of crime. An initial Sh52.5 million portion of that case was closed in March 2023.

A second, linked case involving $3 million held with co-accused firms Adguru and Hupesi Solutions dragged on until November 2023, when the agency withdrew it after what it called further investigation.

The judge who allowed the withdrawal did not simply grant it. He criticised the agency for filing serious proceedings before finishing its own investigation, calling the conduct inappropriate, negligent, reckless and absurd for an investigative body, and ordered that any resulting liability fall personally on the agency&#8217;s director and investigating officer rather than on public funds.

It was inappropriate, negligent, reckless and absurd to an investigative agency.

FlexPay&#8217;s case began with a private business partner, Naivas, taking a contractual payment dispute to the police. Kora&#8217;s and Flutterwave&#8217;s cases began with the state&#8217;s own Asset Recovery Agency acting on its own suspicion of money laundering, with no named commercial counterparty driving the complaint.

What connects all three is the outcome. A Kenyan authority with the power to freeze accounts and arrest executives moved first and investigated the underlying claim afterwards, and in each case the claim did not hold up once examined.

Neither the prosecutors nor the High Court has ruled on whether Naivas or FlexPay is actually owed money, so the Sh31.2 million claim, FlexPay&#8217;s Sh24 million loyalty point counterclaim and its Sh300 million price manipulation allegation all remain unresolved.

Naivas has not said publicly whether it intends to pursue civil proceedings, as prosecutors advised. It is also not yet clear whether the four executives&#8217; constitutional petition will still proceed now that the underlying investigation file has already been closed.

The first thing to watch is whether Naivas actually files a civil claim, since prosecutors advising that route does not guarantee it happens.

The second is whether three reversed cases in four years change how Kenya&#8217;s Asset Recovery Agency and the Directorate of Criminal Investigations screen a complaint against a fintech before freezing accounts or making an arrest, since each case so far has cost the accused company months of frozen funds and public suspicion before the state concluded it had moved too soon.
