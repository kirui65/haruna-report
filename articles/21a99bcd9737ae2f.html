Kenyans Get Court Relief Over 2% Health System Fee as Govt Faces 120-Day Deadline

WhatsAppFacebookX

The High Court has ruled that the 2 percent Health Information Management System (HIMS) fee was introduced without adequate public participation, declaring the charge unconstitutional in a petition filed by Nakuru-based surgeon Dr Benjamin Gikenyi.

Justice Reuben Nyakundi found that the fee, listed in the Third Schedule of the Digital Health (Data Exchange Component) Regulations, 2025, breached Articles 10(2)(a) and 201(a) of the Constitution, as well as Section 5 of the Statutory Instruments Act.

However, the court suspended its declaration of invalidity for 120 days, giving the government time to address the procedural shortcomings. Justice Nyakundi said immediate invalidation could disrupt funding for a system that processes about 26,000 claims daily, potentially affecting health facilities and patients.

&#8220;That a declaration be and is hereby issued decreeing that there is ambiguity in the 2% HIMS system on this charge and the same ought to be revisited on grounds of non-public participation on key sector-wise stakeholders to establish how the 2% fee capped at Kshs 5000 was arrived at and whether it is intergenerational or is capable of being reviewed and at what terms,&#8221; the court ruled.

The court directed the regulation-making authority to subject the HIMS fee to public participation in line with the Constitution and the Statutory Instruments Act within the 120-day period.

Any proposed fee must also come with a regulatory impact statement explaining how the charge was calculated. If the government does not introduce a lawful fee entry within the deadline, the existing entry will lapse.

The court declined to order refunds for fees already collected. It said the charges had been imposed under a published legal instrument that was presumed valid and had not been annulled when the money was collected. It also noted that the fees had paid for services already provided and that refunding payments across hundreds of thousands of claims would be disproportionate to the procedural violation.

The judge questioned whether the 2 per cent fee had received the necessary Senate concurrence, given that the Constitution assigns health services to county governments under the Fourth Schedule.

The court directed State organs to take steps to meet their constitutional obligations and submit monthly progress reports to the High Court’s Deputy Registrar, under the advisory of the Attorney General.

It declined to issue guidelines on government institutions doing business with citizens, saying policy-making on the matter fell outside the court’s role. However, it noted that the Auditor General has an independent constitutional mandate to audit the Department of Health’s accounts, including revenue collected through the HIMS fee.

The court also said personal liability under Article 226(5) would require proof that the unlawful use of public funds caused a loss. The petition had not established such a loss.

The ruling also addressed public institutions’ appointment of private advocates. The court held that law firms engaged by public institutions and county governments do not enter purely private contractual arrangements because those engagements carry a public element.

As a result, such appointments must comply with principles of fairness, transparency, non-arbitrariness, and equal opportunity.

The government now has 120 days to address the legal and procedural concerns surrounding the HIMS fee. If it fails to do so, the current fee entry will lapse.
