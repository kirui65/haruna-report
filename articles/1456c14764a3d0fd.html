Black Friday: Your security doesn’t end at checkout

WhatsAppFacebookX

By Lynette Waweru, Cybersecurity Specialist at ESET East Africa

One of the biggest sales tickets on the Jumia Kenya platform for 2026 is the smart TV alongside smartphones, mobile accessories and kettles. And it is the purchasing of these big ticket items that usually comes with warnings of scams and phishing and fraudsters every Black Friday. Criminals use trust to break into systems, lure shoppers away from real sites and create fake purchases. However, there is another security threat that Kenyan shoppers need to be wary of – what happens after you&#8217;ve bought the TV. A smart TV runs an operating system, connects to a home network and stores streaming logins, and this means it needs the same security awareness as your mobile device or laptop.

There is a good reason for this wariness. The National KE-CIRT/CC cybersecurity report for July to September 2026 names Android TVs as among the top targets of mobile application attacks, together with Android phones, set-top boxes and the Google TV app. The report recorded 177,732 mobile application attacks, and 27,4535,527 web application attacks, over the last quarter. Attackers misused the Android Debug Bridge, a developer feature that should be switched off on consumer devices, and exploited default credentials that had been left unchanged.

After the money has been spent on a top of the range smart TV, or home networking system, it is critical to spend time ensuring that it doesn&#8217;t become a gateway into your home and systems. In November, Google revealed that it had disrupted a botnet of more than 1.3 million Android TV set-top boxes, and attributed this backdoor to the manufacturer that had been licensed to shop Android TV devices with Google apps. According to reports, the manufacturer had embedded the malware in the firmware during manufacturing. While this specific incident involves set-top boxes, it shows that the security of a device balances on trust in the manufacturer, as well as the retailer, and the brand. And this trust can be misplaced at any point along the value chain.

The solution? Shoppers need to ask more questions about their products before they purchase them, regardless of the discounts or the size. It may be a beautiful TV, but if it doesn&#8217;t receive regular security updates or come from a trusted source, then it will be the start of a toxic relationship. Before you spend the money, get the full model number of the TV and look for the manufacturer&#8217;s written security update commitment for that model and find out how long security fixes are guaranteed for. The warranty and security updates are two separate considerations, and you need to know how long the company will support the security of your TV and how you can ensure it remains secured for the foreseeable.

The second check is when you set up the device. During setup, replace any changeable default device or administrator passwords – unchanged passwords are a significant weakness – and when you first connect to the Wi-Fi check for, and install, system updates before adding streaming apps. It&#8217;s worth enabling automatic updates if the model supports these and disable the Android Debug Bridge for now. Only install apps from trusted sources and check the app permissions – on a smart TV that means leaving the developer options switched off, downloading apps from the official store for the TV&#8217;s platform, and declining permissions an app does not need to do its job.

Retailers should also show the minimum security support date for a connected device alongside its price and warranty. It shows consumers that there are security options built-in and also provides a better benchmark when choosing between different models. If one is more protected than another, it is a genuine value-add on purchase. However, until retailers make this standard practice, the responsibility sits with the buyer who should ask for the security date before paying and spend time setting up their system with robust passwords, updates and permissions. A smart TV bought this Black Friday may stay on the home network for many years, and the decisions made now will impact how well it stays protected and how much risk it introduces to the home.
