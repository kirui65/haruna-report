Africa GNSS Lab Turns Its Launch Into a Working Plan in Tunis

WhatsAppFacebookX

Five months after the Africa GNSS Lab and the Africa GNSS Academy were launched in Sousse, the partners behind them came together again, this time to answer a more practical question: how does a shared vision become an institution that actually works? The second workshop took place in Tunis from 11 to 13 November 2026, on the sidelines of the SPACE STAR conference.

Over three days of presentations, debate and closed working sessions, participants focused on the two pillars of the initiative: research, development and innovation on one side and capacity building on the other. The aim was to validate a common strategic framework and gather enough input to finalise an operational roadmap. In other words, the workshop was less about announcing what the Lab wants to be and more about deciding how it will get there.

The room brought together a wide mix of backgrounds. Participants came from 14 African countries, from Senegal, Côte d&#8217;Ivoire and Burkina Faso in the west to Kenya and Tanzania in the east, with Italy also represented. Universities and research centres sat alongside start-ups such as GeoMinds Africa, Rada 360, Tod&#8217;Aers and Yoonir Smart Solution, and specialists in geodesy, remote sensing, artificial intelligence, precision agriculture, aviation and space law.

The SatNav Africa Joint Programme Office coordinated the sessions. Institutions including Senegal&#8217;s Université Amadou Mahtar Mbow and ASPG, Burkina Faso&#8217;s Institut Supérieur d&#8217;Études Spatiales et des Télécommunications, Tunisia&#8217;s Centre for Research in Microelectronics and Nanotechnology and Kenya&#8217;s Regional Centre for Mapping of Resources for Development led or contributed to different strands of the work. The attendees described this diversity as the network&#8217;s greatest asset.

The first day centred on the two committees. The Research, Development and Innovation Committee, led by Université Amadou Mahtar Mbow, set out a vision for the Lab as a hub for applied research, technology transfer and skills development. Its two-year action plan includes implementation of priority pilot projects, pan-African hackathons, innovation competitions and regular webinars, seminars and masterclasses.

The Capacity Building Committee presented the ambitions of the Academy, which will lead training across the network, from school pupils and university students to young professionals, decision-makers and trainers.

Participants endorsed both proposals in principle, with one message repeated throughout: research must answer real needs on the ground, whether those of communities, industry or local authorities. They also called for clearer indicators, stronger global navigation satellite system infrastructure and early outreach in schools. The day ended with an open roundtable on how academia, industry and government can work together.

On the second day, SatNav Africa shared the results of a survey of partners, the first attempt at an inventory of expertise across the network. It showed a multidisciplinary community with experience in training and in securing support from sources such as Erasmus+, Horizon Europe, the World Bank and the African Development Bank.

The discussion then turned to how the Lab will be governed and sustained. The Association Sénégalaise des Professionnels de la Géomatique (ASPG) presented a proposed structure with a Strategic Advisory Council and a permanent Executive Secretariat. Institut Supérieur d&#8217;Études Spatiales et des Télécommunications led the session on resource mobilisation, arguing that backers support projects with clear goals and measurable results. SWAN drew on its experience of Senegal&#8217;s global navigation satellite system reference stations, which have combined public and private support, and TGN suggested starting with a small portfolio of realistic projects.

The attendees were also candid about gaps. Representatives of Institut Supérieur d&#8217;Études Spatiales et des Télécommunications, the Regional Centre for Mapping of Resources for Development and Rada 360 noted that participation remains concentrated in North and West Africa, and urged the Lab to reach more of East, Southern and Central Africa.

The final day moved from strategy to practicalities. Rada 360, a precision agriculture start-up, showed how global navigation satellite systems and satellite data can support smallholder farmers, from mapping plots to guiding tractors, and offered its tools to the Lab&#8217;s pilot projects. The Kenya-based Regional Centre for Mapping of Resources for Development proposed acting as a regional focal point for the Lab and offered to host training events, share its e-learning platform and give partners access to data from its regional global navigation satellite system stations.

A large part of the day was devoted to the draft consortium agreement, which will bind public, private, academic and civil society partners and protect each member&#8217;s ideas and contributions. Participants agreed that Tunisia, through the Centre for Research in Microelectronics and Nanotechnology, could provide a solid legal and administrative foundation for the Lab. Participants also agreed to build communication tools, begin online training on existing partner platforms, and identify the first projects to launch.

The proposed roadmap starts with finalising the committees&#8217; action plans and signing the founding and membership agreements before the end of the year. Early in the new year, the Lab is expected to launch its website and bring its Executive Secretariat into operation, ahead of the selection of the first projects.

For now, the message from Tunis is one of steady progress: partners are aligned on a shared direction, and there is a clear list of next steps to follow.
