Judge orders prisons reforms after inmate was killed with smuggled hammer

The High Court has recommended sweeping security reforms in Kenya's prisons, including mandatory searches of inmates returning from work, tighter control of workshop tools, CCTV surveillance and improved emergency access to locked cells, following the killing of an inmate at Nyeri Maximum Security Prison.

Justice Kizito Magare directed the Kenya Prisons Service to account for measures taken to prevent dangerous tools and other contraband from reaching inmates' sleeping quarters following the death of Shem Mugendi Kariuki, who was attacked with a hammer by a fellow prisoner in August 2015.

The court ordered the Prisons Service to file, within 120 days, an affidavit sworn by an officer of at least the rank of Deputy Commissioner General, detailing measures currently in place to control and account for tools used in prison industries and workshops.

The judge recommended that every inmate returning from work undergo a search before entering accommodation blocks and that prison authorities introduce proper systems for accounting for tools at the end of working hours.

He also said walk-through metal detectors should be installed at entry points to workshops and accommodation blocks in maximum-security facilities.

However, the searches must respect inmates' dignity and decency and be conducted by officers of the same sex, as required under the Persons Deprived of Liberty Act.

The court further recommended installing closed-circuit television cameras in corridors and common areas, improving supervision of accommodation blocks and conducting intelligence-led searches for mobile phones and other prohibited items.

It also called for confidential reporting mechanisms through which inmates can alert prison officers to threats, disputes and potential violence.

The recommendations followed findings that the ward's inmate in charge and another prisoner knew of a dispute between Kariuki and his assailant before the killing.

The judge said prison authorities should review emergency response procedures to ensure officers can gain controlled access to locked accommodation wards when lives are at risk.

In Kariuki's case, night sentries could not immediately access the ward, with several minutes passing before the door was opened.

The court also recommended that every death in prison custody be subjected to a prompt, independent and effective investigation, with the deceased's family informed in writing of the circumstances and findings.

It called for the full operationalisation of the National Coroners Service Act, 2017, and the publication of annual statistics on deaths in custody.

The judge further urged Parliament and the Prisons Service to complete the review of the Prisons Act, 1962, and the Prisons Rules to align them with the 2010 Constitution and the Persons Deprived of Liberty Act.

"In particular, the duty to protect inmates from violence by other inmates, tool and contraband control, and the investigation of deaths in custody should be placed on a clear statutory footing," the judge said.

The court also directed that every prison establishment and the Prisons Service headquarters have a designated information officer to handle requests from families of inmates who die in custody.

The Deputy Registrar was directed to transmit the judgment to the Commissioner General of Prisons, the Kenya National Commission on Human Rights and the Commission on Administrative Justice for their attention to the recommendations.

The reforms followed a petition by Faith Muthoni Kariuki, whose 35-year-old son was killed while serving a 15-year prison sentence.

According to court records, Peter Hiuhu Gachau, alias Undertaker, attacked Kariuki on August 16, 2015, while he was sleeping in Block B, Ward 7.

The court heard that Gachau, who was serving a death sentence for robbery with violence, stole a claw hammer from the prison carpentry workshop, concealed it under his clothes and smuggled it into the ward.

He told investigators that he believed Kariuki had stolen Sh27,000 from his mobile money account.

Gachau struck Kariuki twice on the head before strangling him. He was later sentenced to 35 years in prison.

Justice Magare faulted prison officers for failing to account for tools after work and search inmates before allowing them back into their sleeping quarters.

The court rejected the State's argument that the killing was an independent criminal act by a fellow inmate, ruling that prison authorities had a constitutional duty to protect Kariuki's life and security.

It noted that the ward housed 95 high-risk inmates, many of whom slept on the floor, increasing the risk of violence.

The judge also found that Gachau had been secretly operating a mobile phone inside the prison, which was recovered from another ward after the killing.

The court agreed with lawyer John Mwariri that the government's failure to respond to a letter seeking information on measures taken to ensure prisoners' safety violated the mother's right of access to information under Article 35(1)(a) of the Constitution.

Through Mwariri, Faith Muthoni Kariuki had moved to court seeking compensation, arguing that the State had failed to protect her son while he was in its exclusive custody.

Mr Mwariri submitted that the government had failed to respond to a letter dated May 28, 2025, seeking information on measures taken to enhance prisoners' safety.

The court found that the failure to respond violated her constitutional right of access to information and awarded her Sh2.5 million in compensation.
