Green Tech Hackathon Kenya: 267 Applications, 12 Teams

WhatsAppFacebookX

Oracle says student teams in Kenya built climate and sustainability tools on its cloud and low-code stack at the 2026 Green Tech Hackathon, and the company&#8217;s own write-up carries the headline &#8220;Students drive sustainable innovation with Oracle Technology at the 2026 Green Tech Hackathon in Kenya.&#8221; According to Oracle&#8217;s blog, the event drew 267 applications, narrowed them to 12 finalist teams, and ended with an in-person pitching day at Strathmore University on 16 October 2026. Team Daraja took the title.

A separate report from @iLabAfrica at Strathmore University, which names Oracle Kenya as a partner, fills in what the winners actually built. It describes Team Daraja&#8217;s entry as an AI-powered early-warning tool meant to help farmers anticipate livestock diseases, droughts and floods. That is a concrete, practical product idea, and it is the kind of project a developer audience can judge on its merits.

This story sits on the coding-tools desk because of what the students used and learned. Oracle names Oracle Academy, Oracle APEX and Oracle Cloud Infrastructure as the technology behind the event, and it lists application development, cloud technologies, artificial intelligence and problem-solving as the skills on offer. For readers who track how developers pick up tools, from AI editors (see our Antigravity vs Cursor price comparison ) to low-code platforms, a student hackathon is a useful window into what the next cohort of engineers will reach for first.

The core facts come from two kinds of source. Oracle&#8217;s blog post is the main account, and it frames the event as a program for university and TVET students across Kenya. TVET refers to technical and vocational education and training, so the hackathon was not limited to degree-track computer science students. That matters for the story, because it widens the pool of people who got hands-on time with cloud and application-development tools.

Oracle describes the format as four days of virtual learning, technical mentoring and solution development, followed by the in-person pitching event at Strathmore University. The company identifies Team Daraja as the 2026 Green Tech Hackathon Champions. The @iLabAfrica report, which names @iLabAfrica at Strathmore University and Oracle Kenya, confirms the 12 finalist teams and supplies the description of the winning solution.

An event listing for the hackathon adds the administrative detail. It gives a team size of two to five members, a submission deadline of 30 September 2026, finalist notification on 6 October 2026 and a virtual bootcamp from 12 to 15 October 2026. We treat that listing as a planning document rather than a results report, since it was written before the event ran. One date inside it conflicts with the rest of the record, and we cover that below.

None of the available reports quote a named speaker, a judge or a student. The reports also give no prize amounts, no judging rubric and no list of the 12 finalist teams beyond the winner. We will not fill those gaps with guesses. Where this article reaches past the reported facts, it says so and labels the passage as analysis.

Oracle reports 267 applications for the 2026 Green Tech Hackathon. Twelve teams reached the finals, a figure that both Oracle and the Strathmore @iLabAfrica report confirm. Using only those two numbers, the finalist group is a small fraction of the applicant pool. We will not turn that into a precise acceptance rate, because the reports do not say whether the 267 figure counts individual students or teams. A rate built on an unclear denominator would mislead.

The team-size rule is worth a second look. A cap of five and a floor of two pushes students to split work the way a small product team would, with one person on the data model, another on the interface and another on the pitch. That structure rewards collaboration over solo heroics, and it mirrors how real engineering squads operate.

Oracle describes a two-stage design. Students first spent four days online, learning tools, getting technical mentoring and building solutions. The finalists then met in person at Strathmore University to pitch on 16 October 2026. Splitting the event this way lets a hackathon reach students who live far from Nairobi, since the learning and building phase needs only a laptop and a connection.

The event listing places the virtual bootcamp on 12 to 15 October 2026, which matches Oracle&#8217;s four-day count. Four days is short. A team that arrives with an idea but no cloud experience has to learn a platform, wire up data and produce a demo inside that window. Mentoring is what makes this workable, and Oracle lists technical mentoring as a named part of the program.

Low-code tooling helps here. A platform that generates forms, reports and data screens from a database schema lets a student team spend its limited hours on the problem logic instead of boilerplate. Oracle names APEX as part of the toolkit, and we return to why that choice shapes the projects later in this piece.

The 16 October event at Strathmore University is where Oracle says Team Daraja earned the champion title. Pitching in person tests a skill that code alone cannot show, which is the ability to explain a technical solution to non-technical judges. For a climate-focused event, that skill carries extra weight, because the people who fund or adopt these tools are usually farmers&#8217; cooperatives, county offices or NGOs rather than engineers.

The @iLabAfrica report describes Team Daraja&#8217;s solution as an AI-powered early-warning tool that helps farmers anticipate livestock diseases, droughts and floods. Oracle&#8217;s blog confirms the team as champion but, in the material available to us, the Strathmore account is the one that describes the product. We do not have technical details such as the model type, the data sources, the delivery channel or any accuracy figures, so we cannot say how the system works under the hood.

Even without those details, the problem choice is sensible. Livestock disease, drought and flooding are three hazards that hit smallholder agriculture hard and tend to arrive with some lead-time signal, whether from weather data, soil moisture or reported symptoms in nearby herds. A tool that turns those signals into an early alert fits squarely inside the climate-smart agriculture track that Oracle lists among the event&#8217;s challenges.

Here is our analysis, and it is not a reported fact. Early-warning products live or die on delivery. A prediction that reaches a farmer by SMS or a simple dashboard in time to act is worth more than a more accurate model that sits behind a login nobody opens. When Team Daraja or any follow-on partner takes this beyond a prototype, the interface and the alert channel will matter as much as the machine learning.

The win also fits a pattern in applied-AI education. Student projects that tie a model to a real local hazard tend to stand out against generic demos, because judges can picture an actual user. We cannot confirm what the judges prioritized in Nairobi, since no judging criteria appear in the reports. We can say the winning idea matched the sustainability theme directly.

Oracle names six challenge areas for the 2026 Green Tech Hackathon. They are climate-smart agriculture, waste and the circular economy, carbon reduction, sustainable transport, climate data and water stewardship. The list covers both physical-world problems, such as waste and transport, and data-centric ones, such as climate data. That mix gave teams room to pick a problem that matched their skills.

The reports do not say which tracks drew the most entries, so we cannot rank them by popularity. What we can observe is that nearly every track depends on data collection and reporting, which is exactly what a low-code, database-backed platform handles well. A team building a waste-tracking app or a water-usage dashboard can reach a working demo quickly with that kind of tooling.

Hackathons have worked as recruiting and training tools for well over a decade, and technology firms treat them as a low-cost way to meet students. In Kenya, university innovation hubs such as Strathmore&#8217;s @iLabAfrica have long hosted this kind of event, and they give companies a ready venue, a pool of students and a mentoring network. Oracle&#8217;s choice to partner with a university hub rather than run the event alone follows that established playbook.

Climate-themed hackathons have grown more common as sustainability moved up the corporate agenda. Pairing a green theme with cloud and AI tools lets a vendor show its platform solving problems that governments and donors care about. For students, the theme adds meaning to what could otherwise be a generic coding exercise. We lack figures on how many such events ran in Kenya this year, so we will not size the trend.

Developer culture around tools also shifts fast, and what students learn today may not match what their employers use. Survey data on editor choice shows how slowly some habits change. Our look at Vim, Neovim and Helix usage shares shows long-lived tools holding ground while newer entrants fight for a small slice. Platform vendors know this, which is why they court learners early.

The hackathon&#8217;s edge is the combination of deadline pressure, a live audience and expert help in one place. Its limit is lock-in, since students leave knowing one vendor&#8217;s tools best. A smart organizer balances this by teaching general skills, such as data modeling and cloud basics, that transfer. Oracle lists problem-solving and application development among the targeted skills, which suggests at least some of the learning is portable.

One event will not shift a labor market, and the reports give no hiring or funding outcomes. What 267 applications do show is demand. Hundreds of students wanted four days of cloud and AI training on a sustainability brief, and that is a useful signal for employers, universities and the vendors competing for developer attention.

The TVET angle deserves attention. Open calls that include technical and vocational students bring a different group into the pipeline than events limited to university computer science departments. Employers who need applied builders, rather than researchers, may find that group valuable. This is our reading, and the reports do not break down the applicants by institution type.

For Oracle, the commercial upside is indirect. The company gains goodwill, a base of students who know Oracle Academy, APEX and its cloud, and a story that links its products to climate goals. We have no figures on cost, sponsorship size or conversion to paid usage, so we cannot size the return. The reports contain no prices or product specifications.

Beyond that, local partners gain too. Strathmore&#8217;s @iLabAfrica strengthens its position as a convening point for student innovation, and Oracle Kenya gets a visible presence on campus. Winning teams such as Team Daraja may find doors open to mentors, pilots and funders, although no such follow-up has been announced.

The most uncertain call is the second one. Judges at one event are a small sample, and a different panel could have preferred a waste or transport project. Treat these predictions as informed expectations rather than certainties.

For students, the main lesson is to anchor a project to a named, local problem. Climate hazards affect real farmers, and a solution aimed at a defined user earns attention in a way abstract demos do not. Keep the team to a small group, split roles early and leave time to rehearse the pitch, since the live stage rewards clear explanation.

For educators, the event shows how a hybrid format can stretch reach. A virtual learning phase brings in students from any region, while a single in-person finale keeps the energy and networking of a live event. Pairing the two keeps costs down without losing the stage.

For engineers building AI features, the Team Daraja idea is a reminder to monitor what models do after launch. A prediction service that drifts or fails quietly can mislead the farmers it aims to help.
