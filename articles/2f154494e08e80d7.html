UDA-ODM coalition deal: January 31 deadline set for manifesto team

WhatsAppFacebookX

As ODM&#8217;s Central Committee plots a Coast mobilisation blitz and assures members of fair nominations, the party&#8217;s potential merger with Ruto&#8217;s UDA is moving from political whisper to formal framework.

The Orange Democratic Movement has given its joint manifesto team with the United Democratic Alliance until 31 January 2027 to finalise a report outlining the policy framework that would bind the two parties in a formal political coalition ahead of the August 2027 General Election — a deadline that transforms what many had dismissed as speculative political noise into a structured, time-bound process with real electoral consequences.

The directive was one of four resolutions reached at an ODM Central Committee meeting chaired by party leader Oburu Oginga on Wednesday, as Kenya&#8217;s oldest opposition institution accelerated preparations for the most consequential election it has faced since the death of its founder, the late Raila Odinga. The meeting, held against a backdrop of intense national political repositioning, underscored that ODM is no longer merely watching the 2027 race take shape. It is actively deciding which side of it to occupy.

National Assembly Minority Whip Junet Mohamed, speaking at a press briefing after the meeting, was characteristically direct about what the January deadline means. &#8220;We want to expedite the process of the UDA-ODM manifesto team,&#8221; he said. &#8220;We want that process to be concluded latest by January 31, 2027. We want to receive the report on issues that bind us if we were to form a coalition. We&#8217;re coming together to advance only Kenyans&#8217; agenda.&#8221; The language is careful — &#8220;if we were to form a coalition&#8221; — but the machinery being assembled suggests the conversation has moved well past the conditional.

The significance of a UDA-ODM coalition cannot be overstated in the context of Kenya&#8217;s 2027 political arithmetic. President William Ruto&#8217;s UDA controls the government, the presidency and a substantial share of the national assembly. ODM, despite the seismic disruption of Raila&#8217;s passing, retains a formidable grassroots network, particularly in Nyanza, the Coast and parts of Nairobi. A formal merger of those two political machines would not merely reshape the 2027 race — it would, in all likelihood, define its outcome before a single vote is cast.

Oburu Oginga, who assumed the ODM leadership following his brother Raila&#8217;s death and has moved steadily to consolidate the party&#8217;s internal cohesion, assured members that engagements with UDA would continue through established party structures rather than through the kind of informal backroom arrangements that have undermined previous Kenyan coalition agreements. That assurance matters. Kenya has a long and unhappy history of political coalitions formed on personality rather than policy, dissolving under the weight of unmet promises and personal ambition once the electoral arithmetic changes. A manifesto-driven framework — whatever its political motivations — at least creates a basis for accountability.

Internally, ODM faces its own pressures. The Central Committee used Wednesday&#8217;s meeting to address growing anxiety within the party about the nominations process, with Junet delivering a pointed warning to members inclined to weaponise the issue. &#8220;The party has taken note that people who are not happy with the party should not use the nomination process as a scapegoat,&#8221; he said, &#8220;because assurances have been given through the Central Committee that nominations will be free and fair.&#8221; The warning reflects a reality familiar to any party approaching a general election: the closer the vote, the sharper the internal tensions, and the more attractive the nomination grievance becomes as a vehicle for broader discontent.

ODM Secretary-General Catherine Omanyo sought to project institutional confidence in the face of those pressures. &#8220;I want to assure everyone that ODM is the party to watch,&#8221; she said. &#8220;We are strong and not shaken and used to all manner of waves that come.&#8221; It is the kind of statement that parties make when they need to reassure themselves as much as their members — but Omanyo is not wrong that ODM has survived political storms that would have destroyed less resilient institutions.

The Central Committee also announced a grassroots mobilisation tour of the Coast region from 6 to 22 December 2026, covering every constituency and ward in a region that ODM has historically regarded as core territory but which has shown signs of political restlessness in recent electoral cycles. The timing is deliberate. December mobilisation builds organisational muscle before the January manifesto deadline, ensuring that whatever agreement the UDA-ODM team produces lands in a party that is structurally engaged and locally anchored.

What emerges from all of this is a picture of ODM doing something it has not always done well: preparing methodically, managing its internal contradictions with reasonable discipline, and positioning itself for a negotiation it intends to enter from a position of institutional strength rather than desperation.

Whether the January 31 deadline holds, and whether a UDA-ODM coalition ultimately materialises, will depend on factors that no committee resolution can fully control — among them the ambitions of individual leaders, the demands of regional power brokers, and the unpredictable currents of Kenyan public opinion as 2027 draws near.

But the deadline has been set. In Kenyan politics, that is where everything begins.
