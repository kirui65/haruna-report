IEBC: No money to livestream 55,000 polling stations

WhatsAppFacebookX

Kenya&#8217;s election chief rules out full polling station broadcasts as 2027 campaign season ignites a fresh battle over electoral transparency

Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission Chairperson Erastus Ethekon has ruled out livestreaming proceedings at all 55,000 polling stations during the August 2027 General Election, citing prohibitive costs and insufficient time to procure equipment and train operators before polling day, in a disclosure that has sharpened an already fierce national debate over electoral transparency.

The admission, made at a consultative forum with the media, arrived at a moment of acute national tension. With the August 2027 General Election now less than nine months away, Kenya&#8217;s political temperature is rising fast. Coalitions are being tested, loyalties are shifting, and the institutions entrusted with delivering a credible election are once again at the centre of a storm that is as much about trust as it is about process. For millions of Kenyans who have lived through disputed elections, the question of who sees what, when, and how is never merely technical — it is existential.

Ethekon was unambiguous on the livestreaming question. The costs were too high, the logistics too complex, and the procurement timeline too tight. But he was equally firm on the broader question of transparency, insisting the Commission was concealing nothing and that courts had already settled the legal questions now being recycled as political ammunition.

The results declared at polling stations are final, he said. What follows — collation, tallying and verification at the National Tallying Centre — is administrative work required to determine whether a presidential candidate has secured more than half of all votes cast and at least a quarter of the votes in 24 or more counties. That is a constitutional requirement, rooted in Article 138 of the Constitution of Kenya. It is not, he insisted, a backdoor.

&#8220;I don&#8217;t understand what is all this fuss about the National Tallying Centre,&#8221; he said. &#8220;The courts have said the results at the polling station are final. The rest is secretariat work.&#8221;

The process, Ethekon maintained, would be conducted in full public view, with journalists, political party agents, international media, electoral observers and other stakeholders present throughout. Every stage would be open to scrutiny. &#8220;What more accountability are we asking?&#8221; he posed.

His words carry weight — but also carry the burden of history. Kenya&#8217;s elections have twice ended in catastrophic violence, in 2007 and 2017, with disputed results at their core. The scars have never fully healed. Each election cycle reopens the same wounds: who controls the count, who sees what, and whether the outcome reflects the will of the people or the will of those managing the machinery. It is against that backdrop that Ethekon&#8217;s assurances land, and against that same backdrop that they will be judged. Institutions in Kenya are not evaluated in a vacuum — they are evaluated against everything that came before them.

The political season now under way is unlike any Kenya has seen in recent years. Former Deputy President Rigathi Gachagua&#8217;s Democracy for the Citizens Party is organising aggressively across the Mt Kenya region, reconfiguring loyalties that President William Ruto had long assumed were secure. Opposition figures are sharpening their messaging with renewed urgency. New alliances are forming quietly but deliberately, as politicians calculate which coalitions offer the clearest path to State House or, at minimum, to political survival beyond 2027. The election, analysts across the political spectrum agree, will be decided not only at the ballot box in August but in the months of mobilisation, narrative-building and institutional positioning that precede it — months that have already begun.

It is in that charged context that the debate over the National Tallying Centre must be properly understood. The centre has become a proxy battleground — a place where political actors raise doubts about electoral integrity without necessarily offering evidence of wrongdoing. The strategy is familiar: plant enough uncertainty in enough minds, and the legitimacy of whatever result emerges becomes contestable before a single vote is cast. Ethekon knows this dynamic well. His frustration was evident when he challenged politicians to provide factual information when discussing electoral processes, and urged journalists to move beyond simply amplifying what political personalities choose to say.

&#8220;Not just putting the cameras on their faces and allowing them to spill anything under the sun — some things that do not even make sense — and repeating lies,&#8221; he said, in remarks directed squarely at the media&#8217;s role in the information ecosystem surrounding elections.

He went further, alleging that some of the narratives circulating about the Commission and its processes were being deliberately manufactured by individuals and groups operating both inside and outside Kenya, with the specific aim of influencing public opinion and undermining national stability. The Commission, he said, would not allow unverified claims to go unchallenged, warning that sustained misinformation could discourage Kenyans from registering as voters or participating in the 2027 polls altogether — an outcome that would serve no democratic interest.

Commissioner Ann Nderitu sought to cut through the noise with a pointed question of her own: how would results from tens of thousands of polling stations be collated without a central facility? The question answered itself. She confirmed that Section 39(1G) of the Elections Act, which provides for the live-streaming of results announced at polling stations for public information, remains fully in force and unchanged. No media organisation, she said, has been barred from covering the process at any stage. Political parties that have not complied with requirements on their nomination rules have until December 7 to rectify their submissions, with December 9 marking the hard deadline for the closure of all public fundraising.

Commissioner Dr Alutalala offered what may have been the forum&#8217;s most grounding and, for some, sobering contribution — a direct challenge to the expectation that election results could be announced within an hour given the scale of Kenya&#8217;s electoral infrastructure and the manual nature of its processes. Kenya has approximately 55,000 polling stations spread across 47 counties, ranging from urban centres with reliable infrastructure to remote, hard-to-reach communities where logistics alone present enormous challenges. &#8220;We have 55,000 polling stations, our election is manual, and we are being told that within one hour election results should be announced,&#8221; he said. &#8220;Who bewitched us into believing that?&#8221;

The question was rhetorical. But it pointed at something deeply real: a widening gap between public expectation and operational reality that, if left unaddressed, becomes fertile ground for mistrust. Managing that gap — through honest communication, credible process and sustained public education — is now as important a task for the IEBC as the logistics of running the election itself.

Ethekon said the Commission would address this through sustained media engagement, regular briefings, information sharing and structured journalist training programmes designed to deepen understanding of how electoral processes actually work. A memorandum of understanding signed with the Kenya Media Sector Working Group provides the institutional framework for that cooperation, covering training and accreditation for coverage from individual polling stations all the way through to the National Tallying Centre.

Kenya&#8217;s history demands that every assurance be rigorously tested. Its future demands that every institution be given a fair and unobstructed opportunity to deliver. The IEBC is asking for both. Whether it earns them will depend not on the confidence expressed at media forums, but on what is seen — transparently, verifiably and without interference — when August 2027 finally arrives and every Kenyan vote must be counted and seen to count.
