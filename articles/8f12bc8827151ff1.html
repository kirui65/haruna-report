Sifuna breaks silence on Linda Mwananchi rift

WhatsAppFacebookX

Kenya&#8217;s opposition coalition fractures over party leadership and power-sharing as the 2027 presidential race takes shape.

Nairobi Senator Edwin Sifuna yesterday broke his silence over deepening divisions within the Linda Mwananchi Movement, disputing claims by Embakasi East MP Babu Owino that he had reneged on a power-sharing agreement and directly challenging colleagues who have questioned his fitness to lead the coalition&#8217;s political party ahead of the 2027 General Election.

Speaking in an interview on Royal Media radio stations — Citizen Radio, Mulembé, Sulwe and Vuka FM — Sifuna offered a detailed and pointed account of how the movement&#8217;s leadership arrangements were negotiated, how they unravelled, and why he believes government interference rather than personal betrayal lies at the root of the coalition&#8217;s current troubles. The intervention was significant not simply as a rebuttal but as a signal: Sifuna is not stepping aside, and Linda Mwananchi, whatever its internal tensions, is not dissolving without a fight.

The dispute traces back to the formation of a unified opposition political vehicle following the exit of several leaders from ODM. Sifuna recounted how the group, now homeless politically, began searching for a new party structure. &#8220;We were looking for a new house to be able to ensure that all of us could take shelter in there,&#8221; he said. Five leaders put forward their respective parties for consideration — Babu Owino, Caroli Omondi, Godfrey Osotsi, George Natembeya and Caleb Amisi. A sub-committee was established to submit party documents for legal evaluation, assessing each outfit&#8217;s registration status, structure, compliance and ownership before recommending which one was best placed to serve as the coalition&#8217;s common vehicle.

The group eventually settled on the Equitable Party. Omondi abandoned his own party to join the arrangement, as did former Meru Governor Kawira Mwangaza and Mavoko MP Patrick Makau. Sifuna was then designated party leader and presidential candidate — a decision he maintains was reached collectively and transparently. His challenge to those now questioning that outcome was blunt: &#8220;Is it possible you trust me to be president of the country but you cannot trust me to be party leader of a party?&#8221; The logic, as he framed it, was straightforward. A coalition willing to back him for the highest office in Kenya but unwilling to trust him with party leadership had a credibility problem — and that problem did not belong to him.

Babu&#8217;s account differs. The Embakasi East MP, who has since declared his candidacy for the Nairobi governorship, stated publicly that the original agreement was for Sifuna to be the presidential candidate while he took the position of party leader. &#8220;Initially, we had agreed with Sifuna and other members that the moment we get the party out, Sifuna would be the presidential candidate and I would be the party leader. But things changed. I also don&#8217;t know how things changed,&#8221; Babu said. It is a claim Sifuna does not directly deny so much as contextualise — arguing that the arrangements evolved through collective decision-making rather than unilateral action on his part.

The complication, Sifuna explained, arose when some members raised concerns about the political practicalities of operating under a single party in their home regions. Leaders from Luo Nyanza, in particular, argued that retaining their own parties gave them greater capacity to defend their electoral strongholds and secure parliamentary seats. The group accommodated that position. &#8220;We agreed. If you must be party leader so people enter your party, it&#8217;s okay,&#8221; Sifuna said. The concession, in his telling, was an act of pragmatism — not weakness. But it left the coalition with a structure that is simultaneously unified in purpose and fractured in form.

Sifuna was careful to distinguish between political disagreements and personal estrangement. He insisted that neither Babu, Orengo nor PM Party leader Caleb Amisi had personally confronted him with accusations of betrayal. &#8220;When you talk about rumours, especially these rumours I see on social media, Babu himself or Orengo or Caleb hasn&#8217;t said that Sifuna has wronged him anywhere,&#8221; he said. The charge of betrayal, in his view, is being amplified externally — and he placed that amplification squarely at the door of the government. He accused state actors of deliberately working to fracture the opposition, arguing that the narrative of internal collapse serves those who fear a united front against President William Ruto in 2027.

Whether that explanation fully accounts for the coalition&#8217;s difficulties is debatable. Amisi&#8217;s warning — that if Sifuna is not at the opposition flagbearer table, &#8220;we will break that table&#8221; — does not read as the language of a movement at ease with itself. Orengo&#8217;s decision to shift focus to building his own Liberal Democratic Party, and Babu&#8217;s pivot to the Nairobi governorship race, suggest that the centrifugal forces within Linda Mwananchi are real and not entirely manufactured from outside.

What is clear is that the opposition&#8217;s structural challenge — how to build a coalition broad enough to defeat Ruto while keeping together leaders with competing ambitions, regional constituencies and rival political vehicles — remains unresolved. Sifuna disclosed that the group has held more than 100 meetings in pursuit of a unified party arrangement, without achieving it. &#8220;The hope that Kenyans have placed in Linda Mwananchi does not allow me to have side shows,&#8221; he said. It was part warning to colleagues, part reassurance to supporters — and a reminder that the clock is running.

With August 2027 approaching and a sitting president already mobilising his grassroots machinery, Kenya&#8217;s opposition cannot afford the luxury of prolonged internal theatre. The Linda Mwananchi rift may yet be resolved — Sifuna&#8217;s networks span Western Kenya and beyond, and the coalition retains genuine depth. But unity forged in meeting rooms and radio interviews is not the same as unity demonstrated at the ballot box. For now, the movement&#8217;s leaders are still arguing about the table. The election will come regardless.
