Court overturns Sh4m award to former police officer sacked over suspicious M-Pesa transactions

WhatsAppFacebookX

Vigilance House in Nairobi, the National Police Service headquarters. Court of Appeal has overturned a Sh4 million compensation awarded to a former senior police officer dismissed in 2016.

The Court of Appeal has overturned a Sh4 million compensation awarded to a former senior police officer who was dismissed in 2016 after failing a vetting exercise conducted by the National Police Service Commission (NPSC).

A three-judge bench ruled that Peter Kilonzo Katheka, a former Chief Inspector of Police, was accorded a fair hearing and given an opportunity to explain suspicious M-Pesa transactions that formed the basis of the decision to remove him from the service.

The judges said the transactions, which the Commission considered inconsistent with his declared income, were presented to him during the vetting process, and he was allowed to explain their source.

Katheka, who joined the police service in 1984, told the panel that some of the transactions involved welfare contributions and private business dealings. During a subsequent review, he explained his dealings with a businessman identified as Sammy as loan repayments.

However, the Commission was not satisfied with his explanations and declared him unsuitable for continued service.

“The 1st respondent’s record was before the panel and was considered. We are not persuaded that his removal was disproportionate to the concern established,” the judges ruled.

The former officer had challenged the legality and fairness of the vetting process, arguing that the Commission relied on adverse information that had not been disclosed to him and failed to give adequate reasons for its decision.

He also complained that his long service record was disregarded, the hearing was hostile and the panel was irregularly constituted.

The Employment and Labour Relations Court (ELRC) upheld the Commission's conduct of the vetting but, on its own motion, declared Regulation 13 of the National Police Service (Vetting) Regulations, 2013 unconstitutional. It subsequently awarded Katheka Sh4 million for violation of his constitutional rights.

The Commission challenged the decision, arguing that the judge had improperly invalidated the regulation without inviting the parties to address its constitutionality.

The Court of Appeal agreed, saying the petition had not challenged the validity of Regulation 13 and none of the parties had made submissions on the question.

A customer withdraws money from an M-Pesa shop. Suspicious M-Pesa transactions formed the basis of the decision to remove the former senior police officer from the service.

It faulted the trial judge for introducing the constitutional issue on his own and making it the foundation of the relief granted.

“The gravity of a constitutional question is no licence to determine it in disregard of the very procedural fairness the Constitution guarantees,” the judges said.

The appellate court clarified that its decision did not determine whether Regulation 13 was constitutionally valid, noting that the question would have to be considered in a case where it was properly pleaded and argued.

On the fairness of the vetting exercise, the judges said the Commission's mandate to assess the suitability and integrity of police officers served a legitimate public interest.

They noted that vetting was not a criminal trial and did not automatically attract all the protections under Article 50(2) of the Constitution. However, its administrative nature did not exempt the Commission from complying with Article 47 on fair administrative action.

“We therefore agree with the Commission that vetting is not a criminal trial, and that Article 50(2) is not engaged wholesale merely because the inquiry touches on potentially improper transactions. The converse is equally true: labelling a process administrative does not strip it of the protections of a fair hearing,” the court said.

The judges observed that Katheka appeared before the vetting panel on August 23, 2016, and later sought a review after the Commission declared him unsuitable in a decision communicated to him in December that year.

The review was heard in July 2017 but dismissed the following month.

The Commission maintained that his frequent and substantial M-Pesa transactions with fellow officers and civilians could not be reconciled with his declared earnings, and that his explanations were not supported by the accounts of the people involved.

The former officer, however, argued that he had served for 33 years without blemish and that the Commission's findings were based on conjecture rather than sufficient evidence.

He also maintained that denying him compensation after losing his income, benefits and career prospects would leave him without an effective remedy.

The appellate judges rejected that argument, holding that financial loss could only inform the remedy after a violation of rights had been established.

“His loss of income, benefits and prospects may inform the remedy once liability is established; it cannot establish liability. Nor does the unavailability of reinstatement found an entitlement to constitutional damages,” they said.
