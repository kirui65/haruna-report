Kenya Halves New HIV Infections as Child Treatment Lags

WhatsAppFacebookX

Kenya cut new HIV infections by 56.5 percent, yet 19,434 people died in 2025 as paediatric care lags and US donor transition looms toward 2030.

Kenya cut new HIV infections by 56.5 percent between 2021 and 2025, yet 19,434 people died of AIDS-related causes last year, exposing severe paediatric treatment gaps and rising financial strain as foreign donor support declines.

Ahead of World AIDS Day on December 1, National Syndemic Diseases Control Council (NSDCC) Chief Executive Officer Dr Douglas Bosire and National AIDS and STI Control Programme (NASCOP) head Dr Andrew Mulwa released the 2026 Kenya HIV Estimates. With about 1.4 million Kenyans living with HIV, the data show progress in adult care is masking stalled outcomes for children, youth, and men.

According to the NSDCC estimates, new infections dropped from 32,027 in 2021 to 13,936 in 2025, extending an 82 percent decline since 2015. Even so, the country still logs roughly 38 new infections and 53 preventable HIV-related deaths every day, meaning annual deaths now outnumber new cases by nearly 5,500.

Gender disparities also cut in opposite directions across diagnosis and survival. Women aged 15 and older accounted for 7,363 new infections in 2025, more than double the 3,177 recorded among adult men. Yet men represent nearly half of all HIV-related deaths because lower testing and delayed clinic enrolment leave many untreated until advanced illness.

Adolescents and young adults remain a major transmission driver. Young people aged 15 to 24 accounted for 4,322 new infections, or 41 percent of all adult cases, while children and youth under 25 together represented 55 percent of total new infections nationwide.

The widest gap sits in paediatric HIV care. While the 69,330 Kenyan children aged 14 and younger living with HIV make up 4.7 percent of the total HIV population, they accounted for 2,226 deaths in 2025, or 11.5 percent of national AIDS fatalities. Their AIDS mortality rate stands at 32.1 per 1,000, compared with 12.2 among adults.

NASCOP data show Kenya's paediatric care cascade stands at 79 percent diagnosed, 60 percent on antiretroviral therapy, and 52 percent virally suppressed, far behind the national average of 93, 91, and 87 percent and the global 95-95-95 target. Roughly 14,559 children remain undiagnosed, while 21,908 diagnosed children are not receiving treatment.

Mother-to-child transmission stood at 8.04 percent in 2025, above the global benchmark of under 5 percent. Bosire said 72 percent of the 3,396 new paediatric infections stemmed from pregnant women starting antenatal care late or taking medication inconsistently during pregnancy and breastfeeding, urging male partners to support attendance across four recommended clinic visits.

Sustaining treatment is growing financially harder. Bosire estimated Kenya spends between Sh15,000 and Sh20,000 per patient annually, putting the national drug and care bill at Sh20 billion to Sh25 billion a year. Development partners currently cover about 66 percent of national HIV spending compared with 34 percent from domestic budgets, and the United States expects Kenya to assume full financing by 2030.

Patients are already feeling external aid cuts. Between April and June 2026, supply disruptions left some clinics issuing one- or two-week antiretroviral refills instead of three-month prescriptions, raising transport costs and missed-dose risks. A July 2026 amfAR survey found 73 percent of US-funded Kenyan groups had halted services for key populations such as sex workers, while The Star reported on November 26 that some HIV patients are now paying up to Sh2,000 for diagnostic tests once provided free.

NSDCC is opening World AIDS Day commemorations with a national half marathon at Nyayo National Stadium on November 29 before county-level medical camps and accountability forums on December 1. Alongside faith-sector partnerships targeting adolescent pregnancies and HIV under its Triple Threat campaign, health officials say closing paediatric diagnosis gaps and replacing donor funds in national and county budgets will decide whether recent gains hold.

As AIDS Healthcare Foundation Kenya Country Director Dr Samuel Kinyanjui noted, preventing child HIV deaths is now a test of logistics, clinic retention, and domestic financing rather than medical science.
