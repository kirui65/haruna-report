Nairobi bursary scandal: 4,919 learners received funds up to nine times

WhatsAppFacebookX

Auditor General Nancy Gathungu exposes KSh2.06 billion in bursary irregularities at City Hall — money meant for Kenya&#8217;s most vulnerable students

Auditor General Nancy Gathungu has exposed a systematic looting of Nairobi County&#8217;s bursary and scholarship programme, revealing that 4,919 learners irregularly received multiple allocations — some up to nine times in a single academic year — across a three-year period in which KSh2.06 billion was dispensed without proper financial need assessments, in one of the most damning indictments of devolved governance Kenya has seen in recent years.

The report, covering the financial years ending June 2021 through June 2024, lays bare the mechanics of a scheme designed, ostensibly, to give Kenya&#8217;s most disadvantaged students a path to education. What it delivered instead was a feeding trough: a system so poorly supervised that the same beneficiaries returned to the queue repeatedly, collecting funds that were meant to be distributed once per academic year to genuinely needy learners who, in many cases, never received a shilling.

The core finding is as simple as it is scandalous. Awards were made without any assessment of applicants&#8217; financial needs or social vulnerability — the most elementary requirement of any credible bursary programme. Nairobi County is among seven counties flagged by the Auditor General for bursary irregularities, with KSh301.4 million in unaccounted funds cited separately in the same reporting cycle. But the broader KSh2.06 billion figure represents the full scope of the period under review — money that flowed through Nairobi&#8217;s ward bursary and executive scholarship systems while the oversight mechanisms that should have caught the abuse were either absent or deliberately ignored.

The methodology of the fraud, as reconstructed from the audit findings, is not especially sophisticated — and that is precisely what makes it so troubling. A student or a connected intermediary submits an application. No means test is conducted. No cross-referencing is done against previous awards. The allocation is processed and paid. The same student submits again the following term, or the following year. And again. And again. Up to nine times. The ward office responsible for verification either did not check or chose not to. The supervisory layer above it either did not ask or chose not to notice. By the time KSh2.06 billion had moved through the system across three financial years, the pattern was institutional — not incidental.

Gathungu has previously warned, in her performance audit of bursary programmes nationally, that &#8220;despite multiple education funding mechanisms, many eligible learners continue to be left out as a result of delays, inconsistencies, and weak accountability in the allocation process.&#8221; The Nairobi findings confirm that those warnings were not abstract cautions. They were an accurate description of a system operating as a patronage mechanism rather than a welfare programme — one in which access to funds depended not on demonstrable need but on familiarity with the right people in the right offices.

The Auditor General&#8217;s report on Nairobi County flagged an estimated KSh20 billion in irregular and disputed spending at City Hall across multiple programmes — an astonishing aggregate for a county whose residents include some of the most economically marginalised urban populations in East Africa. The bursary scandal sits within that broader landscape of institutional failure, but it carries a particular moral charge. The Auditor General&#8217;s report for the financial year ending June 2024 detailed how Nairobi City County is rocked with massive irregularities and irregular expenditure amounting to billions of shillings spanning programmes from school feeding to housing to scholarships. The pattern is consistent across all of them: weak internal controls, missing documentation, and a beneficiary selection process that appears to have rewarded proximity to power over demonstrated need.

According to the Auditor General, without proper evidence of beneficiaries, the money may have ended in the pockets of officials managing the funds — a conclusion that transforms what might otherwise be characterised as administrative sloppiness into something far more deliberate. Sloppiness does not pay the same person nine times. Sloppiness does not systematically fail to conduct a needs assessment across 4,919 cases over three consecutive financial years. What the audit describes is a structured extraction of public resources from a programme designed to serve the poor, executed with sufficient consistency to suggest it was understood and accommodated at multiple levels of the county bureaucracy.

What makes the bursary scandal particularly corrosive is precisely the nature of the funds involved. This was not procurement fraud on a government infrastructure contract, where the harm, though real, is diffuse and abstract. This was money designated specifically for children — for school fees, books, uniforms, and the basic costs of remaining in education. Every multiple payment collected by a connected beneficiary or a fraudulent intermediary represents a student who sat at home, fell behind, dropped out, or borrowed against a family&#8217;s already strained resources because the county&#8217;s allocation for genuine need had already been captured by someone gaming the system. The human cost of that arithmetic — played out across thousands of households in Mathare, Kibera, Mukuru and Eastleigh — is immeasurable in audit terms but entirely real in consequence.

The political context surrounding the findings could not be more charged. With Kenya&#8217;s August 2027 General Election now fewer than nine months away, accountability — or its conspicuous absence — has become the defining fault line of competitive politics across the country. Opposition figures have made the misappropriation of devolved funds the centrepiece of their campaign narrative, arguing that billions allocated to counties since 2013 have enriched political elites rather than transformed the lives of ordinary Kenyans. Reports like this one hand that argument its most compelling evidence.

Gathungu&#8217;s audit sampled constituencies across six counties and found, among other systemic problems, stale and unpresented bursary cheques across multiple constituencies — funds allocated but never actually delivered to the students they were intended to reach. In Nairobi&#8217;s case, the problem runs in the opposite direction: money that was delivered — repeatedly — to those who had already received their entitlement, while the genuinely needy queued unserved. Both failures are products of the same underlying dysfunction: a bursary system built without the basic accountability infrastructure needed to ensure public money reaches the right people.

The remedies, in principle, are not complicated. Automation of the bursary application and disbursement process, cross-referencing of national identification details against award histories, mandatory means-testing before any allocation is processed, and real-time digital tracking of payments would, together, make the kind of multiple-payment fraud documented in Nairobi technically impossible to sustain. Gathungu&#8217;s national report noted that South Mugirango, Uriri, Mwala, Gatanga and Emgwen constituencies had fully automated their bursary processes, resulting in improved efficiency and transparency — proof that the solution exists and is affordable. What is missing is not technology. It is the political will to implement it in systems where the opacity of manual processes serves the interests of those who benefit from the current arrangement.

Gathungu&#8217;s reports do not name individual perpetrators. That is the job of the Ethics and Anti-Corruption Commission, the Director of Public Prosecutions, and the county assembly oversight committees to whom the reports are submitted. Whether those institutions choose to act on findings of this magnitude — or whether the reports join Kenya&#8217;s long archive of audit literature that is read, noted and quietly shelved — is the accountability question that matters most in the months ahead.

For the 4,919 learners identified as having received multiple irregular allocations, whatever consequences arrive will follow the slow arc of Kenyan judicial process. For the thousands of genuinely needy students across Nairobi&#8217;s most deprived wards who should have received that KSh2.06 billion and did not, the consequences are already written — in interrupted schooling, abandoned ambitions, and futures narrowed before they ever had the chance to open.

A bursary programme exists for one reason: to ensure that poverty is not destiny. In Nairobi County, between 2021 and 2024, it served a different purpose entirely — and the children who paid the price for that betrayal had no voice in any of it.
