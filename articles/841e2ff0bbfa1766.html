Smartmatic Faces Scrutiny Over Past Election Controversies as Kenya Prepares to Replace KIEMS Kits

WhatsAppFacebookX

A company whose executives have been criminally charged by the United States Department of Justice (DOJ) over alleged bribery and money laundering schemes has reportedly received support from prominent political figures as it seeks to remain involved in Kenya’s electoral technology procurement process ahead of the 2027 elections.

In June 2026, the Ministry of Information, Communication and the Digital Economy released a report detailing an independent technical assessment of the Kenya Integrated Elections Management System (KIEMS) kits. The assessment reportedly recommended the decommissioning and replacement of the existing kits with newer technology designed to minimise obsolescence, reduce long-term costs and accommodate emerging technologies, including artificial intelligence.

Following the assessment, the Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission (IEBC) initiated a procurement process to replace the tablets supplied by Smartmatic.

Smartmatic has, however, faced legal scrutiny in the United States, where the Department of Justice announced criminal charges against the company and senior executives over allegations of conspiracy to violate the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA) and international money laundering.

According to U.S. prosecutors, the alleged scheme involved approximately $1 million in bribes targeting the former chairman of the Philippine Commission on Elections (COMELEC) in connection with multi-million-dollar electoral technology contracts.

Prosecutors alleged that the scheme involved inflating the prices of voting machines and channeling excess funds through purported consulting arrangements and offshore entities in Asia, Europe and the United States to facilitate payments to corrupt officials.

The company's continued involvement in the Kenyan procurement process has also attracted political debate, with former Deputy President Rigathi Gachagua publicly questioning the decision to replace the existing KIEMS technology.

Gachagua reportedly challenged the IEBC's decision to move away from Smartmatic, asking: “Why do we have to remove, to get data from a company that is already in the country and give it a new entrant? What is the gain?”

He also alleged that the proposed transition away from Smartmatic was linked to efforts to manipulate voter registers in particular regions, although the claim was not accompanied by publicly established evidence in the remarks cited.

The debate has raised questions about the influence of political interventions in procurement decisions following the technical assessment recommending the replacement of the existing electoral technology.

Concerns about Smartmatic's track record have also been raised in relation to previous elections in Kenya and other countries.

During the lead-up to Kenya's 2022 elections, three foreign Smartmatic contractors were detained at Jomo Kenyatta International Airport after reportedly carrying sensitive election deployment materials that had not been declared and were allegedly transported without official escorts.

The European Union Election Observation Mission's post-election assessment also highlighted information security weaknesses in the electoral system, including concerns over access controls and the use of shared user accounts that could potentially allow unauthorised access to system functions.

In the Philippines, Smartmatic's electoral technology has faced scrutiny following reported incidents involving corrupted SD cards ahead of the 2019 elections, alleged unauthorised script modifications during vote transmission and a reported security breach involving an employee accused of accepting bribes to facilitate third-party access to secure servers.

The Philippines subsequently barred Smartmatic from participating in its elections, according to reports cited in the allegations against the company.

Other controversies have also been linked to Smartmatic's operations internationally. In El Salvador, reported programming errors involving preferential vote calculations triggered investigations, while in Venezuela, the company's former chief executive publicly alleged that the government had manipulated voter turnout figures by more than one million.

The controversies have renewed scrutiny over the role of electoral technology suppliers, their accountability and the safeguards necessary to protect the integrity of elections.
