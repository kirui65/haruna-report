Engineers blame corruption and regulatory gaps for Kenya’s building collapses

WhatsAppFacebookX

IET-K calls on President Ruto to act as professionals warn that weak oversight is costing lives

The Institution of Engineering Technology of Kenya has called on President William Ruto to urgently address what it describes as serious regulatory gaps fuelling a dangerous rise in building collapses across the country, warning that corruption and government inaction are placing ordinary Kenyans at risk.

Speaking at the 6th IET-K Annual Conference in Mombasa on Saturday, more than 700 engineering professionals expressed alarm at the government&#8217;s failure to operationalise key regulatory bodies responsible for overseeing the construction sector. The gathering underscored the growing frustration within the profession over a crisis that has claimed lives and shaken public confidence in Kenya&#8217;s built environment.

IET-K President Bibiye Mahmoud Juma told delegates that critical institutions — among them the Kenya Engineering Technology Registration Board, the Engineers Board of Kenya, and the Board of Quantity Surveyors and Architects — have yet to be fully constituted, leaving the industry without the professional oversight it urgently needs. Juma urged Ruto to take personal responsibility for ensuring those bodies are properly established and empowered to function. She also called on the Cabinet Secretary in charge of Roads and the National Construction Authority to enforce proper regulation of professionals working on construction sites across the country.

Engineer Lawrence Michemi was blunt in his assessment, describing corruption as the single greatest obstacle to improving safety standards in the industry. He argued that existing loopholes in the regulatory system make it far too easy for individuals to engage in corrupt practices — and that until those gaps are closed, no amount of goodwill will be sufficient. Michemi also stressed the need to build a genuine culture of safety in society, and called for greater use of technology to promote discipline, improve efficiency and strengthen supervision on construction sites.

The warnings come against a backdrop of mounting public anxiety over building collapses, which have become an increasingly visible safety emergency in Kenya. Each incident raises fresh questions about the quality of construction materials, the adequacy of professional supervision, and whether approved building standards are being followed at all.

Professor Michael Saulo, Dean at the Technical University of Mombasa, used the platform to draw attention to the often-overlooked contribution of technicians and technologists in the construction process. While engineers design structures, Saulo explained, it is technicians and technologists who implement those designs on the ground and ensure that the work meets required standards — a distinction that matters enormously when things go wrong.

The conference put forward several concrete proposals. These included declaring building collapses a national disaster, developing clear action plans to improve public safety, strengthening risk management practices across the industry, and accelerating the adoption of technology and innovation to reduce opportunities for corruption and improve monitoring of construction projects.

The professionals were emphatic that lasting change will require more than reacting after buildings fall. They want the government to rebuild regulatory institutions from the ground up, enforce genuine accountability, and ensure that only qualified professionals are permitted to oversee construction work. As pressure on the government intensifies, Kenyans are looking for solutions that go beyond the emergency response — solutions that will protect lives, restore public trust, and guarantee that every structure built in this country is safe to enter.

The message from Mombasa was clear: the safety of buildings must never be sacrificed for financial gain, and the time for half-measures has long passed.
