When elders speak, villages listen: Kenya’s unlikely vaccine champions

WhatsAppFacebookX

Historically the custodians of traditional medicine, village elders are now helping to dispel myths about vaccination and drive-up immunisation coverage.

Beneath the wide branches of an old tree in Kumpa village, in Kenya’s Kajiado county, a group of elderly men and women have gathered. They each find a spot in the shade, and soon, community elder Noah Sampeke signals to the crowd to settle down.

This group typically meets to weigh tradition and custom, bringing it to bear on village disputes. But today’s order of business is about a necessary divergence from tradition.

Like many other rural communities, Kumpa village has historically relied on herbal medicine, with elders like Sampeke acting as custodians of these time-honoured practices. But today, Sampeke is addressing his peers about the importance of immunisation.

... [these elders] really command a lot of respect in the community, so the community members are likely to believe them… They’ve become a very good and effective avenue through which we can conduct advocacy.

- Dr Sultani Matendechero, Senior Deputy Director General for Health at the Ministry of Health

His oratory in Maa, the village’s language, gathers pace; he asks questions, and his audience choruses a response. Before closing, he welcomes community members to raise questions and concerns.

“Every time the doctors inform us about a plan to bring vaccines into our community – like polio or any other – I begin educating the locals about the vaccines. I tell them why they need to accept the vaccines. I do that in forums like this, and also door to door. Some leaders of the clans help me to reach every house,” Sampeke told VaccinesWork.

Superstition and mistrust initially stood in his way. “At first, they did not want to take the children for vaccinations in the clinics because they thought it was meant to infect them with strange diseases, but I tell them that that is incorrect. I tell them that the vaccines prevent the diseases instead. Because they listen to us as their leaders, they embraced the vaccines,” he said.

An estimated 48,000 children in Kenya – many of them in rural areas – do not receive even their first diphtheria, tetanus and pertussis-containing vaccine. To help close this gap, the state has begun involving village elders like Sampeke to address misconceptions at the community level.

“We facilitate the step-down process by providing participants with handouts that they can refer to, ensuring that the key messages are retained and communicated accurately without distortion. We also pair them with Community Health Promoters, who provide ongoing guidance, support and follow-up at the community level. This approach helps strengthen continuity of care and ensures that the information shared reaches the community as intended,” said Dr Sultani Matendechero, Senior Deputy Director General for Health at the Ministry of Health.

I did not understand what vaccines were meant to do because I have always used herbs from the bush to treat diseases from long ago. I also heard different stories from people that made me afraid, but now understand that they talk in ignorance, because the elders have explained about these vaccines. When I have a question, I just ask them. I now understand that vaccines protect us, they protect our children.

Dr Matendechero said that collaborating with the traditional elders is beginning to make a significant difference in how communities respond to vaccines. “They interact with them on a day-to-day basis, and [these elders] really command a lot of respect in the community, so the community members are likely to believe them… They’ve become a very good and effective avenue through which we can conduct advocacy. And also remember that they also have their own gatherings, which we call Baraza. Because those are already established platforms, they become excellent avenues through which we can convey public health messages,” he said.

Such messages have caused residents like Jacinta Naetoi, a mother of four, to reconsider their attitudes to vaccination. Naetoi says accepting vaccines was “not easy”. She grew up seeing her parents using traditional remedies for all sicknesses.

“I did not understand what vaccines were meant to do because I have always used herbs from the bush to treat diseases from long ago. I also heard different stories from people that made me afraid, but now understand that they talk in ignorance, because the elders have explained about these vaccines. When I have a question, I just ask them. I now understand that vaccines protect us, they protect our children.”

Health workers at the local clinic say more local parents are showing up with their children for vaccination since the elders’ intervention. Staff member Josphat Enkare, a ten-year veteran in his role, said such initiatives are also making it easier for health workers to reach out to families without facing hostility.

“We used to keep small volumes of vaccines because the number of people who were coming for them was very small, sometimes none at all. Now they come in large numbers whenever we have a vaccine drive. For the routine vaccines for children, they also move faster. We are also sending community health workers to homes,” he said.

Sampeke, meantime, says seeing children protected makes his efforts worthwhile. “I take it as part of my work because I know that if children are not vaccinated, they might get sickness like polio. We had some who had already been disabled, so I take it as my work to save lives.”
