The Kenyan government has announced plans to demolish Kenya House in New York and construct staff residential units on the same three-acre property, as part of a wider strategy to eliminate a KSh 3 billion annual rent bill for diplomatic premises abroad.

Principal Secretary for Foreign Affairs Abraham Korir Sing&#039;oei revealed the New York redevelopment plan before the National Assembly Committee on Defence, Intelligence, and Foreign Relations on November 23, 2026.

The session was chaired by Belgut MP Nelson Koech, whose committee flagged urgent concerns about underfunding across several Kenyan missions abroad.

Beyond New York, the Ministry of Foreign and Diaspora Affairs has entered a Transaction Advisory Agreement with Rock Investment Bank under a Public-Private Partnership framework.

The arrangement is designed to unlock the value of more than 62 government-owned diplomatic properties globally, while keeping them under public ownership.

Sing&#039;oei signed the agreement alongside Principal Secretary for Public Investments and Assets Management Cyrell Odede and Public Private Partnerships Directorate Director-General Kefa Seda. The PPP Directorate will provide technical oversight in accordance with the Public Private Partnerships Act, 2021, while Rock Investment Bank will carry out feasibility studies, project preparation, and financial structuring during the pilot phase.

The initial phase targets government-owned properties in Cairo, Lusaka, Harare, Bujumbura, Juba, and Kinshasa. Odede underlined the fiscal logic behind the initiative, pointing out that Kenya had long been paying rent on properties it did not own, with no lasting return to the taxpayer.

"The initiative will help ease long-term pressure on the Exchequer while preserving public ownership of strategic national assets," Odede said.

The KSh 3 billion rent bill, which accounts for roughly 15% of the Ministry of Foreign and Diaspora Affairs&#039; recurrent budget, is paid largely in foreign currencies, exposing the government to exchange rate risk.

By developing land it already owns through private-sector financing, the government aims to convert that recurring cost into permanent infrastructure while reducing its dependence on leased premises across Africa and beyond.
