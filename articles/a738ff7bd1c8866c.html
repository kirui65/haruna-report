Sifuna in Maryland: 'Ruto Has No Path to Power as United Opposition Eyes 80% Vote'

Nairobi Senator Edwin Watenya Sifuna addressed Kenyans living in Maryland during his United States diaspora tour, detailing his reluctant role in drafting the previous political agreement with the United Democratic Alliance (UDA). He noted that the pact was originally designed to stabilize the nation and protect human life amid widespread street demonstrations.

"I was the person, unfortunately, who was charged with drafting this agreement that was going to govern this relationship, and I feel that I stayed true to what I believe in," Sifuna said.

According to Sifuna, the agreement contained two non-negotiable pillars: an immediate halt to extrajudicial killings and abductions of youth, and an independent inquiry into the June 2024 protests. He emphasized that the pact explicitly required holding culpable police officers accountable under Kenyan law.

"I wrote into that agreement that we were no longer going to experience abductions and killings of young people. We were going to have an independent review of the happenings of June 2024, that police officers who are responsible for the killings of young people would be held to account in accordance with our laws," he explained.

Sifuna revealed that he publicly declared the agreement dead on television following the death of a young man, Albert Ojwang (referenced in the transcript as Albert/Alberto Jang), who was transported over 400 kilometers to Nairobi and died in police custody. The senator argued that the arrangement lost all value once the state failed to uphold the sanctity of life.

"If we were going to sustain the democratic state, we needed to sustain life, and there was no compromise about that on the day that they killed Albert," Sifuna stated.

Addressing his political split, Sifuna stated that tensions began when certain colleagues misinterpreted the UDA engagement as an opportunity to access state perks and enrich themselves. He maintained that he rejected efforts to fully back President William Ruto's re-election bid, as it was never part of their original understanding.

"There are people who mistook whatever agreement we had with UDA, and they thought it was time for us to get involvement and get comfortable and gorge ourselves on public resources and so on and so forth. I was not of that thinking," he noted.

Responding to critics urging him to soften his tone ahead of the 2027 general election, Sifuna firmly rejected altering his assertive character to appease voters. He argued that staying silent in the face of falsehoods violates his personal principles and party obligations.

"I have honestly before God, I have never been a nice person. I have never been a person to keep quiet when I feel that something is wrong," Sifuna declared.

Sifuna pointed out that the constitution of the Linda Movement mirrors that of the Orange Democratic Movement (ODM), explicitly obligating party leaders to actively counter misinformation. He insisted that transparency and direct confrontation of lies remain central to his leadership style.

"I have never been a person to play games. I don't have cards that I hide under the table. What you see is what you get, and we will engage you," he said.

During the Maryland forum, Sifuna outlined the vision behind the newly established Linda Mwananchi Movement, describing it as a vehicle to steer Kenya away from its current trajectory. He cited assessments from global financial institutions like the World Bank to argue that weak rule of law and democratic backsliding are stifling national progress.

"So we have managed to put together this new movement, the Linda Movement, and we are hoping that we can build it into a formidable force for good in the election that is coming up," Sifuna told attendees.

The senator praised the diaspora audience for openly associating with him, contrasting their freedom with the intimidation faced by citizens back home. He recounted an incident at a Kenyan university where panicked administrators barred him from addressing students due to fears of state retaliation.

"In Kenya right now, people are living in fear. You people are some of the bravest Kenyans I've known because you are able to say, 'We want to come to our event, and you can record like you are free, you are okay being seen with me,'" he remarked.

Sifuna anchored the Linda Movement's platform on the six foundational values enshrined in the preamble of Kenya’s constitution: democracy, freedom, rule of law, equality, and social justice. He stressed that all government development initiatives must strictly adhere to these constitutional guarantees.

"Our entire platform as Linda Movement is a return to those six constitutional values in our preamble," Sifuna affirmed.

Turning to economic grievances, Sifuna identified the high cost of food, healthcare, education, fuel, and electricity as the primary burden on Kenyans. Drawing from his tenure on the Senate Energy Committee, he revealed that 51 shillings out of every 100 shillings spent on electricity goes directly toward taxes.

"You buy a unit of electricity, and if you pay 100 shillings, 51 shillings goes to taxes out of 100 shillings you pay for a unit of electricity," he pointed out.

Sifuna accused Kenya Power of abruptly halting itemized mobile billing breakdowns after the Senate began investigating daily charges, fuel levies, and forex adjustments. He traced high energy costs to corruption embedded in Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) signed during the 2009 El Niño period, noting that a Senate bill requiring disclosure of beneficial owners remains stalled in the National Assembly.

"Because we went and looked at the power purchase agreements that were signed during the advent of El Niño in 2009, and I can tell you for a fact that the problem has always been corruption," Sifuna asserted.

Addressing healthcare reforms, Sifuna noted that his own monthly contribution rose from 900 shillings under NHIF to 24,000 shillings under the new Social Health scheme. However, he faulted the administration for measuring success by registration numbers rather than service quality or ending medical fundraising appeals.

"He measured success of SH by how many people have registered, and I told him SH is not a registration bureau. It's a healthcare scheme, it's supposed to be measured by the quality of services people are receiving," he stated.

Highlighting demographic pressures, Sifuna noted that Kenya’s national and county governments collectively employ fewer than one million people out of a population of 55 million. With roughly one million young graduates entering the job market annually, he urged diaspora professionals to help educate the public on how private sector investments create jobs.

"Government cannot employ all of us. The entire complement of people who work in government at both levels—the national government and the county governments—is not more than a million people," he explained.

Using regional competition as an example, Sifuna warned that multinational companies base investment decisions on overhead costs rather than sentiment. He noted that high electricity tariffs, corporate taxes, employee-employer co-pays, the housing levy, and demands for 30% kickbacks on projects drive investors toward neighbors like Ethiopia.

"If Ethiopia is selling them power at 30 shillings—30 times less the cost of electricity in Kenya—they're going to put the plant in Ethiopia. It's not about sentiment. It's about bottom line," Sifuna said.

Comparing Kenya’s governance transitions, Sifuna observed that the election of President Mwai Kibaki in 2002 immediately shifted public expectations and empowered citizens to reject police bribery without needing new laws. Conversely, he warned that electing leaders facing corruption or court convictions sends a subliminal signal that legitimizes theft of public funds.

"It's a message that comes from who you elect. If you elect a kleptocratic leader, a conman, somebody who has been convicted in courts and faces other charges in various courts including international courts, you cannot expect that person... not to steal from you," he warned.

Discussing coalition strategy ahead of the 2027 elections, Sifuna projected that a united opposition could capture between 75% and 80% of the national vote, leaving President Ruto with no viable path to victory. He referenced American election analysts Steve Kornacki and John King to illustrate how a unified front would dominate Kenya’s electoral map.

"We believe that a united opposition puts us overwhelmingly over the top—estimates of anywhere upwards of 75% to 80% of the vote. Simply put, William R by the way has no path to power," Sifuna predicted.

Sifuna disclosed that opposition unity negotiations are anchored on two fundamental questions: why Kenyans want the current administration removed, and what immediate reforms will follow once power changes hands. He reported to the diaspora gathering that opposition leaders have made substantial progress toward reaching consensus on both fronts.

"First, what in your view is the reason Kenyans are saying R must go? Question number one. [Second], is the country that Kenyans want to see immediately he goes," he outlined.

Cautioning his opposition allies against complacency, Sifuna emphasized that Kenyan voters will demand tangible governance changes within three months of a new administration taking office. He criticized extravagant displays of wealth by current officials—such as wearing Rolex watches and using helicopters to transport pets—while ordinary citizens struggle to afford food.

"Even in three months, [if] they don't see any recognizable change in the way the affairs of the country are, they will be back in the street singing 'Saba Saba must go,'" Sifuna cautioned.

Citing Auditor General findings and economic experts, Sifuna claimed that between 500 billion and 1.8 trillion shillings is lost annually to budgeted corruption. He called for a complete national reset across both the executive and parliament, urging Kenyans to abandon the culture of lobbying influential figures for government tenders.

"All of you here must stop making those calls to people in influence to help you with tenders and other things. We must all as Kenyans stop this habit of trying to just get ahead by the money equation," he urged.

Referencing World Bank projections, Sifuna stated that curbing corruption could unlock 8.8 million meaningful jobs for Kenyan youth and propel annual economic growth from a sluggish 4% into double digits of up to 25%. Acknowledging his background as a lawyer rather than an economist, he pledged to restore meritocracy and tap into diaspora expertise to execute the movement's manifesto.

"It's okay for leaders to acknowledge that they don't know everything. I am a lawyer... I am not a finance guru, I am not an economic expert, but there exist this expertise," Sifuna admitted.

In his last remarks, Sifuna dismissed claims that a presidential campaign requires 6 billion shillings, noting that his movement relies on organic support rather than paid crowds or printed merchandise. He appealed to the diaspora for logistical backing, technical expertise, and volunteer polling agents to safeguard the vote.

"The money that we need is just for logistics—for me to be able to be present in order for counties, to pay for fuel, for vehicles, and for people to be able to even just have lunch and leave," Sifuna explained.
